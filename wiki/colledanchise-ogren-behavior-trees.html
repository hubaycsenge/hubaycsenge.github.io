The PDF in raw/ is arXiv:1709.00084v6, dated 25 October 2022, printed from a browser. It is not the CRC Press print edition, and it is four years newer than the published book. Cite by chapter and section, not page number. Replaces the 45-page preview clipped earlier the same day.

Why it is here: Csenge's decision, 2026-07-28 — behaviour trees as the executive layer for decisions made by reinforcement learning algorithms. This book is the standard reference for BTs and, in Chapter 8, contains the specific literature on combining them with RL.

Takeaways

  • A BT is a switching structure, not a state machine. Internal nodes are control flow nodes (Sequence →, Fallback ?, Parallel ⇒, Decorator); leaves are execution nodes (Action, Condition). The root emits ticks at a frequency; a node executes only if ticked, and returns Running, Success or Failure to its parent.
  • The central argument is two-way control transfer. FSM transitions are one-way control transfers — Dijkstra's goto. Reactivity requires many transitions; many one-way transfers destroy modularity, because removing a component means revising every transition into it. BTs use two-way transfers governed by internal nodes, so a leaf is written without knowing what runs next. This is the whole book in one idea.
  • BTs generalise FSMs, hierarchical FSMs, the Subsumption architecture, Teleo-Reactive programs and Decision Trees (Ch. 2, formalised in Ch. 6).
  • Chapter 8 is the reason this source matters here — see below.
  • Analysis tooling exists: Ch. 5 gives a state-space formalism for safety, robustness and efficiency; Ch. 9 converts Stochastic BTs into Discrete Time Markov Chains and computes Mean Time To Succeed, Mean Time To Fail and success probability over time, and shows these compose across subtrees.
  • Honest about limits: §2.6.2 lists real disadvantages (below).

Chapter 8 — the RL integration, and what it says about this thesis

Two learning approaches, and the comparison between them (§8.4) is the load-bearing passage.

RL-BT (§8.3) — start from a manually designed BT and replace a subset of nodes with Learning Nodes, of two kinds:

  • Learning Action node — encapsulates Q-learning with user-defined state, action set and reward. Their example: an Action "pick an object" learns grasping, where state is object pose, actions are grasp poses, reward is grasp quality.
  • Learning Control Flow node — a Fallback with flexible child ordering. Given a state, it learns the order in which to try its own children. Reward can be task-dependent or simply return-value-dependent (positive for Success, negative for Failure).

GP-BT (§8.2) — genetic programming builds and extends the BT itself, starting from a single Action Learn node, greedily trying single actions first and invoking GP only when no single action improves the reward.

The architectural justification for the whole plan

§8.4, comparing the two:

"If RL-BT was started from scratch with a BT consisting of a single action, there would be no difference between RL-BT and standard RL. Instead, the point of combining BTs with RL is to address the curse of dimensionality, and do RL on smaller state spaces, while the BT connects these subproblems in a modular way."

This is a stronger claim than "the tree executes what the policy decides". The BT is not downstream plumbing — it decomposes the learning problem. Each learning node faces a small, local state space; the tree supplies the structure that would otherwise have to be learnt.

That matters for rl-for-behaviour-learning, which argued the layering on sutton-barto-2018-rl §14.3 (A(s) as an ethologically-authored action set). The two arguments compose: the ethology supplies which primitives exist, and the BT supplies how the learning problem is carved up between them. Neither source makes the other's point, and together they are a much better justification than either alone.

The cost, stated by the authors: RL-BT "needs user input regarding both the BT structure, and the actions, states and local reward functions to be considered in the subproblems", whereas GP-BT needs only a single global reward plus the available conditions and actions. More hand-authoring, not less — which for this thesis is an advantage, since hand-authored structure is where the ethology lives (fuzzy-automaton-fri).

Their RL-BT experiment is a search-and-rescue toy: rooms containing victims and one of three fire types, three extinguishers each effective against one fire type, assignment unknown to the agent. Scenario 1 (one Learning Action node) converges to 100% accuracy; Scenario 2 (nested learning action and learning control-flow nodes, with time-consuming actions) converges to 97–99%, the shortfall attributed to the control-flow node needing trial and error to learn the ordering. 30 episodes × 400 iterations. A simulated toy problem, not a robot — the robotic validation in Ch. 8 is for GP-BT (KUKA Youbot), not RL-BT.

Two further results worth carrying

The safety subtree. GP-BT runs Sequence(T_safe, T_learn), where T_safe is hand-authored and overrides the learned tree whenever needed; the authors note this "is the only part of GP-BT that requires user input", and that unsafe regions need a conservative margin so the safety action can act in time (§8.2, §5.3).

That is a concrete mechanism for the problem sutton-barto-2018-rl §17.5 names and does not solve — "safe embedding of reinforcement learning agents into physical environments". For a robot learning around untrained humans in a real environment, this is directly load-bearing. See rl-for-behaviour-learning.

FSMs scale badly under learning. Comparing GP-BT against an FSM-based learner and pure GP on the Mario AI benchmark, "the FSM approach generates a number of nodes that increases exponentially over time, while the growth rate in GP-BT tends to decrease over time" (§8.2.4.1, Fig. 8.8). §2.6 makes the same point analytically: "a FSM with a large number of transitions can drastically deteriorate the speed of an algorithm that has to consider all the possible paths".

This is a quantitative argument bearing on Q9 — fuzzy-automaton-fri is a state machine, and the corpus' reason for choosing it was hand-authored readability, not learnability. See behaviour-trees.

Design principles worth reusing (Ch. 3)

Explicit success conditions for readability; implicit sequences for reactivity (check the goal first, then preconditions, rather than checking outcomes); decision-tree structure for case handling; Sequence nodes to guarantee safety; backchaining to build deliberative BTs; memory nodes when un-reactive behaviour is wanted; and choosing the right granularity.

The safety discussion includes a caution the wiki should keep: a naive safety sequence "will probably lead to an unwanted chattering behaviour" — recharge to just over 20%, resume, drop to 20%, repeat.

Limitations and disadvantages

The authors' own list (§2.6.2), which is unusually candid:

  • The BT engine is complex to implement — ticks should be generated and traversed in parallel with action execution.
  • Checking all conditions can be expensive, sometimes infeasible; closed-loop execution then costs more than it gains.
  • Feed-forward execution is sometimes just fine — in structured, predictable environments BTs have no advantage over simpler architectures.
  • BTs require a new mindset: "the execution of BTs is not focused on states but on conditions and the switching is not event driven but tick driven".
  • Tooling is less mature than for FSMs.

For this wiki specifically:

  • Almost nothing on social robotics or HRI. §1.6 surveys autonomous driving, industrial robotics, the Amazon Picking Challenge and — in a single paragraph — the social robot JIBO, noted only as having a BT-based SDK. No interaction design, no human-subject results, no affect. The coverage gap recorded in citation-backlog as "behaviour trees in HRI — nothing" is only partly closed: this book supplies the architecture, not its social application.
  • No emotion content at all. The emotion-plus-BT reference the thesis needs is still Belle, Gittens & Graham (2019), which is not held.
  • Chapters 5, 6, 7 and 9 were read for scope, not in detail — their results are recorded here by what they deliver, not derived. If a formal guarantee is to be claimed in the thesis, re-read the relevant chapter properly first.
