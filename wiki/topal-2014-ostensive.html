The theoretical frame

Human communication is ostensive-inferential. Following Sperber & Wilson's Relevance Theory and Csibra & Gergely's Natural Pedagogy, ostensive signals are verbal and non-verbal cues that give evidence of the communicator's intention to convey information, distinct from the information itself. Csibra's key point, quoted here: ostensive cues do not work merely by grabbing attention — if they did, "any loud noise" would serve — but because the mind is wired to interpret them as a precursor to communication addressed to oneself.

Natural Pedagogy claims this system enables fast transfer of generic, cognitively opaque knowledge in one or a few exposures, where non-human observational learning is slow and context-bound. Their contrast table (11-1) is worth having:

Non-human social/observational learning Human learning via pedagogy
Novice's role passive (inter)active
Focus of attention uncontrolled guided by referential signals
Source of information inflexible (dominance, kinship) flexible (expression of communicative intent)
Validity of information context-embedded generalisable
Presentations needed several one or few
Timing based on present needs flexible
Imitation of opaque elements rarely frequently

What dogs actually do — the four conclusions

The chapter's own summary of the eye-contact, verbal-addressing and combined-cue literature (§11.3.1.3), which is the most citable passage:

  1. Dogs treat human ostensive signals as indicating that they are being instructed — not as mere discriminative cues — and this is "often indispensable" to their responding to referential gestures.
  2. Eye contact is the strongest ostensive signal. High-pitched vocalisation ("doggerel", the analogue of motherese) is secondary but important. Calling the dog by name is of minor importance — Kaminski et al. (2012) found dogs respond the same to their own name and an unfamiliar one.
  3. Ostensively cued behaviour "can often act as imperatives", inducing a "ready-to-obey" attitude. No comparable responsiveness in hand-reared wolves.
  4. Non-ostensive attention-getters also affect responses, but dogs' specific responsiveness rests on perceiving communicative intention.

The finding that should worry a robot designer

Ostensive cueing makes dogs follow bad instructions

"Dogs show a strong initial bias towards maladaptive response in object choice tasks if the inefficient or mistaken solution is cued ostensively by a human." Dogs follow deceptive pointing despite having seen the reward hidden elsewhere (Szetei et al. 2003); select an empty container despite having watched the correct one baited (Erdőhegyi et al. 2007; Topál et al. 2009a); adopt inefficient object-manipulation strategies after repeated demonstration (Kupán et al. 2011); and abandon their natural preference for the larger food quantity when a human ostensively prefers the smaller (Prato-Previde et al. 2008).

Human children show the same bias. Dogs can learn to override it (Elgier et al. 2009).

For this thesis: if the robot is designed to be dog-like and responsive to ostensive cueing, it inherits a documented failure mode — ostensively delivered instructions override the robot's own evidence. That is a safety-relevant property for a robot operating in a real environment, and it is also, read the other way, exactly the mechanism that makes shaping work (reward-design). The same sensitivity that makes an agent trainable makes it manipulable.

What dogs do not do

Dogs meet two of three criteria for being a recipient in pedagogical knowledge transfer. They are sensitive to ostensive cues, and they comprehend the referential character of human cueing. But:

Human ostensive-communicative referential signals seem insufficient to create an 'infant-like' expectation to receive generic information about objects or events in dogs. That is, whereas in infants ostensive signals induce a specific receptive attitude towards learning something generic, it seems that in dogs, an ostensive context induces a 'ready-to-learn' or 'ready-to-obey' attitude that is largely tied to the here and now.

The authors' reinterpretation of the adaptive function follows, and it is the most useful sentence in the chapter for interspecific-hri:

The adaptive function of dogs' interspecific communicative skills can be conceptualised as not (only) knowledge acquisition from human partners but as facilitating behavioural synchronisation in order to avoid conflicts and/or to co-act in terms of common actions without necessarily comprehending the causal structure of the collaborative interaction.

And the minimum specification, which reads almost as an engineering requirement — the skills that constitute socially competent responding in ostensive situations are the ability to (1) monitor, (2) gain, (3) maintain and (4) shift attention while interacting with humans, and (5) share a common focus with the partner.

They are explicit that none of this requires mind-reading: "dogs as well as infants can grasp the explicit or overt dimension of communication without any sophisticated mind-reading abilities or conceptual thinking skills", and "we are not necessarily entitled to call this phenomenon 'recognition' of intention".

The missing third criterion is exactly what the engine is being designed around — 2026-07-30

The infant version of that third criterion is now held as topal-2008-perseverative, by four of the same authors: ostensive framing alone flips 10-month-olds from encoding "the object is now under A" to encoding "this kind of object lives in A", raising perseverative error from 0.48 to 0.81 with the manual demonstration held identical.

Csenge's specified pipeline — perceive ostensive cues → infer pedagogical intent → reinterpret pragmatically rather than as sampled behaviour — is that third criterion. This chapter says dogs do not have it.

Not a contradiction; the same authors draw the contrast on purpose. But it means the mechanism the emotional engine is being built around is an infant competence, not a dog one, and thesis step 4 leans on the dog as the model species at precisely this point. Recorded as Q20; three possible resolutions on pedagogical-stance.

The evidence that would settle it is this chapter's own citation, Topál et al. (2009a) in §11.3.1.3 — the dog A-not-B result, almost certainly the direct counterpart of topal-2008-perseverative. Not held, and now the top item in citation-backlog.

Other findings worth keeping

  • Left gaze bias. Dogs show it towards human faces (neutral, positive and negative), but towards dog images only when the expression is threatening (Guo et al. 2009; Racca et al. 2012). Left gaze bias is associated with right-hemisphere processing of facial-emotional information.
  • Breed and genetics. Cooperative working breeds and short-nosed breeds are better at visually guided cooperative interaction involving eye contact; gazing behaviour in an unsolvable task is associated with dopamine receptor gene polymorphism (Hori et al. 2013).
  • Eye contact is ambivalent. Between dogs it signals dominance and ritualised aggression; with unfamiliar humans in a threatening approach it evokes fear and raises heart rate (Vas et al. 2005; Gácsi et al. 2013). The communicative reading is an interspecific special case, described as an infant-like predisposition overriding a wolf-like aversion. 2026-07-30 — and its primacy is not universal. In 9-month-old infants, direct gaze alone produced gaze-following but no referential object learning, while infant-directed speech produced both (okumura-2020-ostension-attention). The ordering may be species-specific, or an artefact of that study's video stimuli — a recorded face cannot make contingent eye contact. Both readings are set out on that page. Consequence for the build: do not assume eye contact is the cue to implement first. Q21.
  • Social referencing and contagion: dogs use human emotional information about a novel object to guide their own behaviour (Merola et al. 2012); yawn contagion is reported (Silva et al. 2013).

Limitations

  • A review chapter, not new data; every empirical claim is second-hand.
  • Inside the network. Same institutions as most of the corpus — see hungarian-ethorobotics-network. The authors do note the debate (Udell et al. 2009 and "contrasting theories of the origins of dog social cognition"), which is more even-handedness than most of the corpus offers, but the opposing literature is still not in the wiki. #needs-source
  • Nothing about robots. The transfer to an artificial agent is this thesis' work, not the chapter's.
