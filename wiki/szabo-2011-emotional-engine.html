The model

Grounding: explicitly Panksepp (panksepp-affective-systems). The paper names the circuits — SEEKING-expectation, FEAR-self-defence, RAGE-anger, LUST-sexuality, CARE-parental attachment, PANIC-grief/sadness/separation, PLAY-joy/happiness — and states: "We applied Panksepp's model to build our core emotional engine." Hence several parallel, equal basic emotional states representing distinct brain circuitry, changing independently in response to external events.

States: five — happiness, despair, fear, anger, surprise (surprise added relative to the 2010 version's four).

Input model: an expert-elicited table mapping 13 environmental contexts (greeting owner, leaving owner, petted, owner initiates interaction, owner scolds/punishes, rejected interaction initiation, meeting familiar person, ambivalent social situation, facing threatening stranger, play with human, threatening stimulus, strange environment, unpleasant inner state) onto each emotional state via arousal (5-step) and valence (±).

Dynamics — a second table gives per-emotion temporal character: width of emotional peak, decline of short-term emotion, impact of short-term on medium-term, decline of medium-term. E.g. fear has a very broad peak, slow decline, high impact on medium-term; surprise is very short and declines fast.

Mathematics: discrete state-space, two time layers.

xR[k+1] = A·xR[k] + B·u[k]      yR[k] = C·xR[k]        (short-term)
xM[k+1] = N·yR[k] + M·xM[k]     yM[k] = O·xM[k]        (medium-term)

Linear core, extended non-linearly: B computed dynamically from the new saturation level, desired emotion width, A, and an anti-windup mechanism; C saturates to keep outputs in [−1,1]. Medium-term layer is the "mood". Asymptotic stability enforced by choice of A and M.

Emotion arbitration — the part the wiki did not have

Against Kismet's winner-take-all, Szabó et al. define a hierarchical rule tree (emotion-arbitration):

  1. One positive dimension → express it.
  2. Multiple positive dimensions: a. fear > 3 (medium) → express fear regardless of everything else; b. one dimension strictly highest → express it; c. ties broken by fixed hierarchy: fear > surprise > anger > happiness = despair; d. ties at ≥ 3 → the top two are expressed as mixed or alternating behaviour.

Justified via MacLean's triune-brain hierarchy — which the authors explicitly flag as obsolete in light of newer neurobiology, while arguing it remains useful for ordering emotions. Mixed emotions here means ambivalent behaviour across modalities (e.g. submissive posture + aggressive vocalisation), not Plutchik's secondary/tertiary dyads — the paper cites plutchik-model only to distinguish itself from it.

Validated in MATLAB with a GUI, on a scripted scenario (greet → pet → play → rejected interaction → owner leaves). Arbitration output: Happiness → Surprise → Despair → Neutral.

Limitations

  • Simulation only. No robot, no sensors, no human subjects. "The robot shown in Figure 5 was chosen... We are currently working on the design of simple behaviours."
  • Inputs are hand-labelled discrete events, not sensor-derived. The entire perception problem is assumed away — precisely where multimodal-emotion-recognition would have to sit.
  • Expert-elicited tables from "extensive causal observations", not measured data. The 2010 paper admits "there are no experimental data on dogs which could help choose the particular time constants".
  • Medium-term layer is not integrated into arbitration ("further investigation is needed").
  • No robot internal state. Battery, motor and sensor health appear nowhere.

Where this leaves the thesis

Two of the three claimed contributions are already occupied: a Panksepp-based engine exists, and dog-derived behaviour is established. What remains genuinely open, and is strengthened rather than weakened by this paper:

  1. Real sensor input instead of scripted events.
  2. Robot internal state as a second input stream (Q2 in open-questions).
  3. Real-time operation in real environments with human validation (Q3).
