The two experimental paradigms of animal learning, and their correspondence with the two algorithm families of reinforcement learning. All of this page is from sutton-barto-2018-rl Ch. 14 unless marked otherwise; the primary sources it reviews are not in the corpus (see citation-backlog).

This page exists because the dog behaviour this thesis models is largely conditioned behaviour, and rl-for-behaviour-learning needs the vocabulary.

The correspondence

Psychology Reinforcement learning
Classical (Pavlovian) conditioning Prediction — policy evaluation, learning a value function
Instrumental (operant) conditioning Control — policy improvement

The defining difference between the two paradigms is whether the reinforcing stimulus is contingent on the animal's behaviour. In classical conditioning it is not; in instrumental conditioning it is.

The authors are careful that this is "a convenient first approximation" and not an identity: "There is more to classical conditioning than prediction; it also involves action, and so is a mode of control, sometimes called Pavlovian control" (§14.1). Both kinds of learning are likely engaged in most real situations.

"Control" means the opposite of what ethologists mean by it

In this book control means the agent influences its environment. In psychology it usually means the animal's behaviour is controlled by the stimuli it receives (stimulus control). Both directions are at play; the book's focus is the agent as controller (§14 terminology). Worth watching when reading this alongside the ethology sources.

Classical conditioning

Pavlov's terms, still standard: an unconditioned stimulus (US) naturally triggers an unconditioned response (UR); a neutral stimulus that reliably precedes the US becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) and comes to trigger a conditioned response (CR). In delay conditioning the CS extends through the interstimulus interval; in trace conditioning the CS ends before the US begins.

Two phenomena drove the modelling literature:

  • Blocking — if an animal has already learnt that a tone predicts the US, adding a light to form a compound CS produces little or no learning to the light. This refuted the idea that temporal contiguity alone is sufficient for conditioning (Kamin 1968, via §14.2.1).
  • Higher-order conditioning — a previously conditioned CS can itself act as a US for a further stimulus. Pavlov's dog learnt to salivate to a black square that had only ever been followed by a metronome, in ten trials. Hard to demonstrate above second order, because the higher-order reinforcer extinguishes when it is no longer followed by the original US.

The corresponding notion in instrumental conditioning is the conditioned (or secondary) reinforcer: a stimulus that consistently predicts primary reinforcement becomes reinforcing itself. Primary reinforcement is that whose "rewarding or penalizing quality has been built into the animal by evolution" (§14.2.1). This is the mechanism by which a value function can supply immediate feedback when the real reward is delayed — see td-learning and actor-critic.

Models. The Rescorla–Wagner model (1972) explains blocking by making learning proportional to prediction error — "an animal only learns when events violate its expectations". Formally it is the Least Mean Square / Widrow–Hoff rule (§14.2.2). The TD model generalises it from trial-level to real time and additionally accounts for higher-order conditioning and for the timing of responses within a trial; see td-learning.

Instrumental conditioning

Thorndike's puzzle-box experiments and the Law of Effect: successful actions are "stamped in", unsuccessful ones "stamped out". Sutton & Barto identify two features shared with RL algorithms (§14.3):

  • selectional — try alternatives and select among them by consequence;
  • associative — the selected alternatives are attached to particular states.

"Natural selection in evolution is a prime example of a selectional process, but it is not associative...; supervised learning is associative, but it is not selectional."

Instinctual impulses and the action set

The passage that matters most for this thesis:

The cats selected actions from those that they instinctively perform in their current situation, which Thorndike called their "instinctual impulses." First placed in a puzzle box, a cat instinctively scratches, claws, and bites with great energy... Successful actions are selected from these and not from every possible action or activity. This is like the feature of our formalism where the action selected from a state s belongs to a set of admissible actions, A(s). Specifying these sets is an important aspect of reinforcement learning because it can radically simplify learning. They are like an animal's instinctual impulses. — sutton-barto-2018-rl §14.3

The design consequence is developed in rl-for-behaviour-learning: the ethologically-derived behaviour repertoire in dog-human-interaction-model can be A(s), rather than being replaced by learning.

Shaping

Skinner's technique of reinforcing successive approximations to a target behaviour — the pigeon-bowling anecdote in §14.3. Sutton & Barto stress that it is "not only indispensable for animal training, it is also an effective tool for training reinforcement learning agents", and return to it in §17.4 as the principal answer to sparse reward. See reward-design.

Directly relevant here: social-competence lists responsiveness to learning and training among the dog-derived properties a robot should have (miklosi-2017-ethorobotics). Shaping is the ethologically-native form of that training, and it is also a legitimate RL technique. The two requirements coincide.

Motivation

The book links psychology's notion of motivation to RL in two places (§14.3):

  • What is rewarding is state-dependent, and the state "not only includes information about what is external to the machine... but also what is internal to this machine". "An animal will be more rewarded by eating when it is hungry than when it has just finished a satisfying meal."
  • Value functions are the proximal motive — an agent selecting actions by value is ascending the gradient of its value function.

The first is a formal hook for the robot's own internal state in emotion-engine — internal state as a modulator of the reward function rather than as another input dimension. Note what the book does not cover: the vigour of behaviour after learning (a hungry rat runs faster). It says this "does not link so cleanly" and points to other literature.

Delayed reinforcement

The credit-assignment problem, in Minsky's (1961) phrasing. Two mechanisms in the book, both with antecedents in animal-learning theory (§14.4):

  • Eligibility traces — decaying traces of past state visits or state–action pairs, matching Pavlov's stimulus traces and Hull's "molar stimulus traces". Introduced to RL by Klopf (1972).
  • Value functions learnt by TD, providing near-immediate evaluation, matching Hull's account of conditioned reinforcement passing backwards from the goal.
