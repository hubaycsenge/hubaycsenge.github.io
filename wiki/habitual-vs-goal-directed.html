Psychology's distinction between two modes of behavioural control, and its correspondence with RL's model-free/model-based distinction. From sutton-barto-2018-rl §14.5–14.6 and §15.11.

Psychology Reinforcement learning
Habitual — triggered by antecedent stimuli, performed more or less automatically Model-free — decisions read out of a cached policy or action-value function
Goal-directed — controlled by knowledge of the value of goals and of the relationship between actions and consequences Model-based — decisions made by planning with an environment model

An environment model has two parts: a state-transition part and a reward part. What psychologists call a cognitive map (Tolman 1948) is the same thing — and, crucially, it "can be learned by supervised learning methods without relying on reward signals", then used later when motivation appears. That is the account of latent learning: rats that explored an unrewarded maze caught up almost immediately once food was introduced.

The trade-off

Goal-directed control adapts fast to a changed environment, because changing the model changes the policy through planning, without new experience of the affected states. Model-free control cannot: "for a model-free agent to change the action its policy specifies for a state... it has to move to that state, act from it, possibly many times, and experience the consequences of its actions" (§14.6).

Habitual control is faster to execute and more robust once the environment is stable. "The development of goal-directed behavioral control was likely a major advance in the evolution of animal intelligence."

Overtraining converts goal-directed into habitual

The experimental evidence is outcome devaluation. Adams & Dickinson (1981) trained rats to lever-press for sucrose pellets, then devalued the pellets by pairing free access to them with a nausea-inducing injection. In subsequent extinction trials the injected rats lever-pressed less from the very first trial — without ever having experienced lever-pressing followed by sickness. They had combined knowledge of the outcome with the outcome's changed value: model- based control.

Adams (1982) then compared rats trained to 100 rewarded presses against an overtrained group at 500. Devaluation strongly suppressed pressing in the first group and had "little effect" in the overtrained group — "if anything, it made it more vigorous". Extended training had converted goal-directed behaviour into a habit.

Daw, Niv & Dayan (2005) explain the shift by confidence-weighted arbitration: both processes run, each proposes an action, and the more trustworthy one wins. Early on the model-based process is more trustworthy because chaining short-term predictions needs less experience; later the model-free process wins because planning accumulates model error and shortcuts like tree-pruning.

The authors note that not every psychologist accepts the cognitive reading of devaluation experiments, "but the model-based planning explanation is widely accepted".

In the brain

Lesion and imaging work (§15.11) places model-free processes more in the dorsolateral striatum and model-based in the dorsomedial, with the orbitofrontal cortex implicated in the reward part of the model and the hippocampus in the state-transition part — when a rat pauses at a choice point, hippocampal activity "sweeps forward (and not backwards) along the possible paths", and the trajectories correspond to what the rat then does.

But the separation is not clean. Doll, Simon & Daw (2012), quoted in §15.11: "model-based influences appear ubiquitous more or less wherever the brain processes reward information" — including in the dopamine signals themselves. This qualifies the tidy actor–critic story in actor-critic.

Why this page exists

The corpus' central untested construct now has a mechanism

social-competence is defined by miklosi-2017-ethorobotics as something that emerges over time through interaction, and thesis records that no study in the corpus tests it, because every study is single-session.

This chapter supplies a documented, quantified process by which repeated interaction changes the mode of behavioural control — and an experimental instrument for detecting it, outcome devaluation, which measures whether behaviour is still sensitive to the value of its consequences.

A robot that shifted from deliberate to habitual responding as a relationship developed would be a concrete, measurable claim about partnership formation rather than a restatement of the framework. Whether that is a good idea for validation-study is open — it would require a longitudinal design the corpus has no precedent for. Q16 in open-questions.
