The RL architecture that maps most cleanly onto both the mammalian brain and the structure this thesis already has. From sutton-barto-2018-rl §13.5, §15.7–15.8 and §15.10.

The structure

Two components learning simultaneously from the same signal:

  • The critic learns a state-value function for whatever policy the actor is currently following, using a TD method (td-learning).
  • The actor holds the policy and updates it from the critic's TD error. "A positive δ means that the action was 'good' because it led to a state with a better-than-expected value; a negative δ means that the action was 'bad'."

The critic has no direct access to the action; the actor has no direct access to the reward. The same δ reinforces both, but with opposite objectives: the actor works to keep δ as positive as possible; the critic works to drive |δ| toward zero (§15.8).

The only structural difference between actor and critic is the

eligibility trace The critic's traces are non-contingent (presynaptic activity only) — making its learning rule "essentially the TD model of classical conditioning". The actor's are contingent (they also depend on the unit's own output) — making its rule "like instrumental conditioning using a Law-of-Effect-type learning rule". "The difference between the critic and actor learning rules is relatively simple, but this difference has a profound effect on learning and is essential to how the actor–critic algorithm works." (§15.8)

This is the same prediction/control split as conditioning, realised as one line of algebra.

The critic solves the delayed-reward problem for the actor: "the TD error produced by the critic acts as a conditioned reinforcement signal for the actor, providing an immediate evaluation of performance even when the primary reward signal itself is considerably delayed" (§14.4).

The neural hypothesis

Following Takahashi, Schoenbaum & Niv (2008), §15.7 places the actor in the dorsal striatum and the critic's value-learning in the ventral striatum, with dopamine neurons in the VTA and SNpc broadcasting δ to the corticostriatal synapses of both.

Three facts of neuroanatomy make this fit: dopamine axons target both striatal subdivisions; dopamine appears critical to synaptic plasticity in both; and the effect of a neuromodulator depends on the target structure, so one broadcast signal can drive two different learning rules. Dopamine neurons are unusually well suited to broadcasting a scalar: each axon makes roughly 500,000 synaptic contacts, 100–1,000 times more than a typical neuron.

Two cautions the authors give:

  • Modern evidence points to "the more complicated picture that different subpopulations of dopamine neurons respond to input differently", i.e. the signal may be vector-valued rather than scalar (§15.4).
  • Lesion work shows the actor placement is "too simple": the dorsolateral striatum is more involved in model-free/habitual processes, the dorsomedial in model-based/goal-directed ones (§15.11). See habitual-vs-goal-directed.

Teams, and what it says about arbitration

§15.10 treats the actor as a population of independent learning units all receiving the same broadcast δ — a team problem in the game-theoretic sense: no conflict of interest, one common signal that evaluates the collective action.

The result: if each unit's algorithm can learn despite a reward signal heavily corrupted by the noise of the other units' contributions, and despite only partially observing the state, the population as a whole improves — without the units communicating with one another. Williams (1992) showed that a team of Bernoulli-logistic REINFORCE units performs policy gradient ascent on the team's common reward, even when interconnected into a multilayer network. The authors note this is "a neurally plausible alternative to the widely-used error-backpropagation method", if a slower one.

Two requirements for it to work:

  1. Contingent eligibility traces — non-contingent traces "do not work at all in the team setting" because they give no way to correlate an individual action with the change in the shared signal.
  2. Persistent variability in each unit's output, so the team explores the collective action space.

A third option for emotion-arbitration

That page currently frames the choice as winner-take-all (Kismet) versus hierarchical rule tree (szabo-2011-emotional-engine). §15.10 supplies a third: a set of behaviour units under one broadcast evaluative signal, with the expressed behaviour emerging from their collective output rather than from an explicit selection rule.

It also reframes winner-take-all, which the book treats not as a naïve default but as a deliberate simplification of structural credit assignment: "An extreme case is a winner-take-all arrangement (for example, the result of lateral inhibition in the brain) that restricts collective actions to those to which only one, or a few, team members contribute. In this case the winners get the credit or blame."

Under a learning regime that matters concretely: a rule tree gives no way to attribute outcome to component behaviours, whereas winner-take-all does. The arbitration question and the credit-assignment question turn out to be the same question. (Inference from §15.10 applied to this wiki, not stated in the book.)

Why this architecture and not another

For the behaviour-learning task in rl-for-behaviour-learning, actor–critic has three properties that matter here, all from the sections above:

  • It separates prediction from control, which lets the emotional state model (prediction of what events mean) stay distinct from the behaviour policy (what to do) — the split emotion-engine already has.
  • It handles delayed and sparse reinforcement through the critic, which is the regime of any real social interaction.
  • It is the architecture the brain plausibly uses, which for a thesis in the ethorobotics tradition is an argument of the same kind as the dog model itself — biological plausibility as design justification.

Against it: the book's own §17.5 caveats about online incremental learning apply, and actor–critic inherits them.
