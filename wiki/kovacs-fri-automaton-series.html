Four papers, one contribution

These four are a conference series with heavily overlapping text — the same FRI fuzzy automaton, the same MogiRobi worked example, largely the same literature review. They are filed as one source page because separating them would imply four distinct contributions. Cite the specific venue when the distinction matters:

  • SYROCO'09 — Interpolation based Fuzzy Automaton for Human-Robot Interaction. First statement.
  • SAMI 2010 — Fuzzy Automaton based Human-Robot Interaction.
  • HSI 2011 — Ethologically Inspired Robot Behavior Implementation. Adds the behaviour-based control decomposition.
  • CogInfoCom 2011 — A novel, ethologically inspired HRI model implementation: Simulating dog-human attachment. Frames the work around the Strange Situation Test.

The problem they solve

Ethological models are descriptive verbal models: a sparse collection of observed action–reaction rules, not a complete specification. Two consequences:

  1. A state machine is the natural structure, since the models follow observed event sequences; and states are continuous, so a fuzzy automaton.
  2. But a classical fuzzy rule base is exponential in the state-vector length and input dimension — R = n·iⁿ·jᵐ. For 10–20 state variables that is thousands of rules, while the actual ethological knowledge is a few dozen.

Fuzzy Rule Interpolation (FRI) resolves the contradiction: the rule base may be sparse, and FRI still yields a reasonable interpolated conclusion when no rule fires. Their FIVE method uses the Vague Environment concept and scaling functions to turn fuzzy interpolation into crisp interpolation, then applies Shepard-operator interpolation for cheap multidimensional evaluation. See fuzzy-automaton-fri.

Two properties they emphasise, both directly relevant to this thesis:

  • Low computational demand — "quick enough to fit the requirements of real-time embedded applications". A real-time claim, though never benchmarked.
  • Human-readable rules — even after numerical optimisation, "the rules are still human readable, and helps the formal validation of the model by the ethological experts". A genuine advantage over a learned model.

The worked example

MogiRobi in an unfamiliar room with Owner and Stranger, modelling the Strange Situation Test (attachment-sst). Fuzzy states: MissTheOwner, AnxietyLevel, ExplorationLevel. Sample rules:

If OwnerInTheRoom=False Then MissTheOwner=Increasing
If OwnerToDogDistance=High And StrangerToDogDistance=Small
   Then AnxietyLevel=Increasing
If AnxietyLevel=High Then ExplorationLevel=Low

Run: owner leaves at step 17, stranger enters at 31 → anxiety and missing-the-owner rise, room exploration is abandoned.

The output function ω: S×X→Y decomposes into parallel component behaviours plus behaviour fusion, giving the standard behaviour-based control structure.

Relationship to the emotional engine

Two separate architectures in the same research programme

This series and the Szabó engine are parallel, not integrated. Kovács et al. model behaviour state (anxiety, exploration, missing-the-owner) with a fuzzy automaton; Szabó et al. model emotional state (happiness, fear, anger, despair, surprise) with a state-space model. Neither paper set cites the other's architecture as a component. Integrating them is an open opportunity — see Q9 in open-questions.

Limitations

  • The worked example is a "tiny fragment" of a rule base, repeated verbatim across all four papers. No complete model is published.
  • No evaluation of any kind — no accuracy, no timing, no user study. The real-time claim is asserted from the method's cheapness, never measured.
  • No emotion recognition: the automaton's inputs are geometric (distances, presence flags), not affective.
  • Rule bases are hand-authored from expert knowledge.
