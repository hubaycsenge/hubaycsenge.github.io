József Topál, György Gergely, Ádám Miklósi, Ágnes Erdőhegyi and Gergely Csibra, Infants' Perseverative Search Errors Are Induced by Pragmatic Misinterpretation, Science 321(5897):1831–1833, 26 September 2008. doi:10.1126/science.1161925.

Peer-reviewed primary report, three pages plus Supporting Online Material (the SOM is not in the held PDF — see Provenance below).

This is the experimental core of the pipeline Csenge specified on 2026-07-30: perceive ostensive cues → infer that the demonstration is pedagogically intended → reinterpret it pragmatically rather than as sampled behaviour. The paper is the demonstration that the third step is real, measurable, and dissociable by manipulating only the ostensive cues. See pedagogical-stance.

The claim

The A-not-B (perseverative search) error is standardly explained as a failure of inhibitory control, short-term memory, visuomotor response bias, or motor simulation via the mirror-neuron system (refs 7–12 of the paper). The authors propose instead that it is a pragmatic misinterpretation: the infant reads the ostensively demonstrated hiding as a teaching demonstration and therefore encodes a generic proposition rather than an episodic one.

The two available readings of the same hiding event, in the paper's own words:

Reading Proposition Consequence in B-trials
Episodic (correct) "the target object is now under container A" search at B — no error
Generic (incorrect) "this type of object is usually found in container A" / "we keep toys in container A" perseverate at A

The generic reading is what Csibra & Gergely call the interpretive bias of generalizability, induced by a receptive "pedagogical learning stance". The prediction: strip out the ostensive signals and the error should drop, even though the motor and visual experience of the A-trials is unchanged.

Design

Three groups of 10-month-olds, 14 per group (N = 42). Four A-trials then three B-trials; a 4 s delay between hiding and the containers being pushed within reach.

Condition What the demonstrator did
OC — ostensive-communicative Eye contact, smiling, infant-directed speech ("Hello baby, look here!"), gaze shifting back and forth between infant and container while hiding
NC — noncommunicative Face and torso turned 90° away; hands equally visible during the identical hiding actions; never looked at or communicated with the infant
NS — nonsocial Demonstrator behind a curtain; only the object's movement visible

The NC condition is the load-bearing one: it holds the visual and motor experience of the A-trials constant and removes only the communicative framing.

Results

Proportion of correct searches:

Condition A-trials B-trials Within-condition drop
OC 0.88 0.19 t₁₃ = 8.917, p < 0.0001
NC 0.80 0.52 t₁₃ = 2.536, p = 0.025
NS 0.78 0.59 t₁₃ = 1.96, p = 0.072 (n.s.)

Two-way ANOVA (phase × context): phase F₁,₃₉ = 49.376, p < 0.0001; interaction F₂,₃₉ = 8.041, p = 0.001. A-trials did not differ across contexts (F₂,₃₉ = 1.525, p = 0.23); B-trials did (F₂,₃₉ = 6.660, p = 0.005), with OC worst (Tukey– Cramer: OC vs NC p < 0.05, OC vs NS p < 0.01).

Infants committing the error (≥2 errors in 3 B-trials), χ² = 8.265, p = 0.016:

Condition Committed the error
OC 12/14 (86%)
NC 6/14 (43%)
NS 5/14 (36%)

The abstract's "81 to 41%" is the OC-versus-NS contrast

Those are B-trial error rates (1 − correct): OC 0.81, NC 0.48, NS 0.41. The headline figure therefore compares the ostensive condition against the nonsocial one. The theoretically decisive comparison is OC vs NC — same manual demonstration, no communication — which is 0.81 vs 0.48. Cite whichever, but say which.

What the design rules out

  • Motor priming / inhibition failure: OC and NC gave identical motor and visual experience of repeated hiding at A, and identical repeated search responses. Only the framing differed.
  • Covert imitation via the mirror-neuron system (ref 12): same argument — the manual hiding actions directed at A were equally observable in both.

What the authors concede

Recorded because the wiki should not overstate this source:

  • The error was reduced, not abolished (43% and 36% still committed it), "which suggests that infants' search behavior also depends on their inhibitory, information processing, and memory skills".
  • Memory decay is a necessary component: "Had we not inserted a 4-s delay between hiding and searching, infants would have been likely to search for the toy at the correct location."
  • The ostensive condition "may have generated higher cognitive load".

Their reply to the last two: load and decay accounts predict random search, and only OC produced above-chance perseveration; NC and NS sat near 50%. The proposed mechanism for the asymmetry is that the communicative demonstration lays down a semantic (potentially long-term) memory trace, and that the primacy effect of semantic learning, against the recency effect on episodic memory, makes the new location hard to relearn.

Why this matters for the thesis

The switch is what the robot needs, and it is a switch, not a gradient

The same demonstration, the same motor experience, the same object — and the learner extracts a different kind of proposition depending on whether ostensive cues were present. That is exactly the third stage of Csenge's pipeline, and this paper is the evidence that it exists as a discrete interpretive mode rather than as an intensity effect on attention. The attention half is separately excluded by okumura-2020-ostension-attention.

For rl-for-behaviour-learning the translation is direct and consequential: an ostensively framed observation should not be consumed as one sample with the usual learning rate, but as evidence about a generalisable regularity — high weight, one or few exposures, persisting rather than decaying. See pedagogical-stance and reward-design.

The mechanism is documented by its failure

Nothing here shows the pedagogical stance producing a correct inference. The entire result is the stance misfiring — a bias that "normally helps infants learn from demonstrations but misleads them in the context of a hiding game".

A robot built to switch into a generic-encoding mode on ostensive cue inherits the failure mode along with the capability, and the dog literature already documents the same thing from the other side: deceptive pointing, empty containers, smaller food quantities (topal-2014-ostensive §11.3.1.3, which cites Topál et al. 2009a for the dog A-not-B result). Trainability and manipulability remain the same property (ostensive-signalling).

Limitations

  • N = 14 per cell, single lab, single age (10 months). No replication in the held PDF.
  • Inside the same Budapest network as most of the corpus. Topál, Gergely, Miklósi and Erdőhegyi span the Hungarian Academy of Sciences, CEU and ELTE — and Miklósi is the author of miklosi-2017-ethorobotics. This is not independent evidence relative to hungarian-ethorobotics-network; Csibra is at Birkbeck. Independent corroboration of the pedagogical-stance claim comes instead from okumura-2020-ostension-attention (Kyoto/Osaka), which is why the two are best cited together.
  • The OC condition bundles eye contact, smiling, infant-directed speech and gaze alternation. Nothing here says which cue does the work, or how many are needed. okumura-2020-ostension-attention isolates IDS; neither isolates eye contact.
  • Infants, not dogs. The generalisation to a dog-modelled robot is not this paper's, and topal-2014-ostensive explicitly denies dogs form the generic expectation. That gap is Q20 in open-questions and is the sharpest consequence of this ingest.
  • The Supporting Online Material — which holds the Materials and Methods — is not in the held file, so procedural detail beyond the main text is unverified.
