The Ortony–Clore–Collins model: emotions as valenced reactions to construals of the world, differentiated by the cognitive conditions under which they emerge. Primary source ortony-2022-cognitive-structure (2nd edn, 2022); reached second-hand in this corpus through belle-2019-programming-with-affect, which uses the 1988 emotion set.

The third emotion model in the wiki, alongside panksepp-affective-systems and plutchik-model — and the one affective computing actually builds on. It sits on a different axis from both: see emotion-model-comparison.

The three branches

Everything starts from an undifferentiated valenced feeling. Focusing on one aspect of a situation shapes it into a specific type (Fig. 2.1, p. 29):

Focus Appraised against Central variable Undifferentiated state
Events and their consequences goals desirability satisfied / dissatisfied
Agents' actions standards praiseworthiness approving / disapproving
Objects tastes appeal liking / disliking

Focusing need not be conscious — it means only that cognitive resources are being deployed, which OCC notes is often bottom-up and unaware.

The 24 emotion types

Six groups. Positive types first, negative second. Names are labels for structural descriptions, not definitions — OCC is explicit that it is not defining English words.

Group Branch Types
Well-being events Joy · Distress
Fortunes-of-others events affecting others Happy-for · Schadenfreude · Resentment · Sorry-for
Prospect-based envisaged events Hope · Fear · Gratification · Relief · Fears-confirmed · Disappointment
Attribution agents' actions Admiration · Pride · Reproach · Self-reproach
Attraction objects Affection · Appreciation · Enmity · Distaste
Compounds events + actions Gratitude · Self-satisfaction · Anger · Self-anger

Structure within a group is simple and regular; structure between groups is not. OCC argues emotions "cannot be arranged informatively into any single space of reasonably low dimensionality" — an explicit rejection of the dimensional approach, and therefore of the PAD space that belle-2019-programming-with-affect layers on top of OCC's own output.

Two distinctions carry weight for this thesis:

  • Fortunes-of-others requires a model of what is desirable for someone else, independent of what is desirable for oneself. A robot that has this can be sorry-for a person; one that cannot has only Well-being emotions about its own goals.
  • Attraction splits on whether the object is construed as emotion-capable — Affection/Enmity for emotion-capable objects, Appreciation/Distaste otherwise. OCC names domestic robots as a case where people personify an inanimate object, and gives responsiveness and apparent reciprocity as the criterion. See emotion-attribution and ortony-2022-cognitive-structure.

Intensity

Intensity is treated as a first-class part of the theory rather than an afterthought — a deliberate contrast with the theories OCC was reacting to, and expanded further in the second edition.

Global variables (Ch. 4, p. 72 ff.) affect intensity across all groups:

Variable What it captures
sense-of-reality how real the event/agent/object seems to the experiencer
psychological proximity felt closeness to the emotion-inducing situation
unexpectedness the familiar surprise term
arousal pre-existing arousal at the time

Local variables act within particular groups — effort and realisation for event-based emotions, familiarity for attraction emotions, expectation-deviation for attribution emotions. Both kinds usually work through the central variables: effort raises desirability, familiarity raises appeal.

sense-of-reality is the interesting one for a robot. OCC introduces it to explain emotional numbing, vicarious emotion, and why merely imagining a situation does not reliably produce an emotion. A robot reasoning about reported versus sensed events needs exactly this distinction.

The computational formalism

Ch. 9 (pp. 215–228) gives rules in two stages. For "Joy" emotions:

Rule 1:  IF  DESIRABILITY(p, e, t) > 0
         THEN set JOY-POTENTIAL(p, e, t)
              = f_joy[ |DESIRABILITY(p, e, t)| , I_global(p, e, t) ]

Rule 2:  IF  JOY-POTENTIAL(p, e, t) > JOY-THRESHOLD(p, t)
         THEN set JOY-INTENSITY(p, e, t)
              = JOY-POTENTIAL(p, e, t) − JOY-THRESHOLD(p, t)
         ELSE reset JOY-INTENSITY(p, e, t) = 0

Where p is a person, e a construed event, t a time, and I_global the combined effect of operative global intensity variables. "Distress" is structurally identical with a negative desirability and f_distress.

Three properties worth carrying into emotion-engine:

  1. Potential and intensity are separate quantities. An event can generate potential without producing an emotion.
  2. The threshold is per-person and per-time — THRESHOLD(p, t), not a constant. It is where individual difference and current state enter the model.
  3. Sub-threshold potential need not be discarded. OCC notes the current value of potential "can always be preserved" even when intensity is zeroed — which is how accumulation and slow burn get represented.

This is a candidate answer for arbitration, not just for generation

emotion-arbitration asks which emotion gets expressed when several are active. A per-emotion threshold with preserved sub-threshold potential is a third option alongside winner-take-all and the rule tree: emotions compete by how far past their own thresholds they are, which normalises across types that are not otherwise commensurable. Untested, and OCC does not propose it for this purpose — recorded here as a design option, not a finding. #needs-source

OCC's own framing of what a formalisation is for should be kept in view: a system that predicts and explains human emotions, not one that has them. See machine-emotion-question.

Relation to the other two models

Briefly here; the full comparison is on emotion-model-comparison.

OCC panksepp-affective-systems plutchik-model
Grounding cognitive appraisal neural circuits evolutionary adaptation
Organising principle construal type brain system functional prototype
Representation 24 discrete types, hierarchical 7 circuits 8 prototypes, cone
Intensity first-class, global + local variables not modelled cone axis
Input it needs events, goals, standards, tastes stimuli + homeostatic state stimuli
Behaviour mapping none approach/avoid tendencies explicit behavioural column
Used by affective computing heavily rarely occasionally

The important asymmetry: OCC models the front end, the other two model the states. OCC says almost nothing about what an emotion does once it exists — there is no behavioural column, no action tendency, no expression. Panksepp and Plutchik say comparatively little about how a situation produces one state rather than another. They are not straightforwardly rivals, which is why Q1 is not simply a three-way choice.

Open issues for this thesis

  • No behavioural output. OCC differentiates emotions but does not say what a robot should do in each. dog-human-interaction-model would still be needed.
  • Goals, standards and tastes must be represented explicitly. OCC's appraisal layer presupposes a value system the robot holds. On robot-platform that is currently implicit in the behaviour tree. Gratch and Marsella's argument that appraisals are read off quantities a competent planner already computes is the way in — see appraisal-theory.
  • Attribution emotions need responsibility attribution, which needs a model of other agents' intentions. Almost certainly out of scope; Well-being, Prospect-based and Attraction are the tractable groups.
  • Anthropocentric value systems. Whether "standards" mean anything for an interspecific-hri partner is untested. Q5.
