The question it settles

Two theories had been fighting over what ostensive cues actually do:

  • Natural pedagogy theory (Csibra & Gergely) — ostensive cues signal that relevant, generalisable knowledge is about to be transferred, and switch the infant into a receptive learning mode.
  • Attention modulation theory (Szufnarowska et al. 2014; Gredebäck et al. 2018; De Bordes et al. 2013) — ostensive cues have no special communicative function; they simply raise attentional arousal. Evidence: non-ostensive attention-grabbers (shivering) elicit gaze-following just as well as direct gaze.

The authors' diagnosis of the impasse: every prior study measured only gaze-following, which is an orienting response. If ostension does something extra, it will show up in what is learned about the object, not in whether the head turns.

So they separated the two levels within one paradigm:

Level Measure
Gaze-following (orienting) proportion of first looks to the target; proportion of looking duration at the target
Referential object learning object processing — novelty preference for the non-target at test, indicating the target was encoded; object preference — which of two real objects the infant reaches for

Design

N = 140 9-month-olds, 20 per condition, seven conditions across four experiments. Tobii TX300 eye tracker, video stimuli, six gaze-shift trials, then two test trials. Reaching coded by a blind coder, κ = 1.00.

Condition Cue before the gaze shift Class
Gaze-only direct gaze only baseline
Shivering rapid horizontal head shakes, ~25° attentional
IDS infant-directed speech, "Hello baby!" ostensive
IDS (replication, Exp 1b) as above, new group ostensive
No cue (Exp 2) downward gaze, no direct gaze control
Beep (Exp 3) nonverbal beep, duration and volume matched to the IDS attentional, auditory modality controlled
Mouth-moving beep (Exp 4) beep plus the actor's mouth moving as in IDS attentional, visual saliency controlled too

Experiments 3 and 4 are what make this decisive: they strip the ostensive content out of the IDS while holding the auditory channel and then the visual channel constant.

Results

Every cue raised looking at the actor's face, and every cue produced gaze-following. Only IDS produced learning.

Post hoc on object processing: IDS differs from Gaze-only (p = .012) and from Shivering (p = .043); Gaze-only and Shivering do not differ from each other (p > .250). Object preference across the first three conditions: χ²(2) = 7.06, p < .05.

What this establishes

Attention-grabbing and ostension dissociate — and they dissociate at the learning stage

The authors' own summary: "although attention enhancement shifts the attention toward the target, ostensive communication also increases infant motivation to encode the target object because ostension is understood as intentional referential communication."

Both camps were right about different things. Gaze-following is driven by attentional arousal, as the attention-modulation people said. Referential learning is not, as the natural-pedagogy people said. The controversy was an artefact of measuring only the orienting response.

This is the experimental backing for a claim ostensive-signalling has been carrying since 2026-07-28 on Csibra's assertion alone, quoted second-hand through topal-2014-ostensive: that ostensive cues do not work by grabbing attention, because if they did "any loud noise" would serve. Experiment 3 is that loud noise, and it does not serve.

This gives the ostension detector a falsification test

A robot module that claims to detect ostension must fire on infant/dog-directed speech and not on a beep of matched duration and volume. If it fires on the beep it is a salience detector wearing the wrong label, and the pipeline downstream of it will switch into pedagogical mode on any loud event in the room.

That is a cheap, concrete acceptance test for the first stage of Csenge's pipeline, and it comes with a matched stimulus pair already specified. See pedagogical-stance and Q21 in open-questions.

The finding that threatens the outbound direction

Infants follow a robot's gaze but do not learn from it

Reported here second-hand, from the authors' own earlier work: "previous studies demonstrated similar findings that human gaze, but not robot gaze, facilitates object learning in 12-month-olds (Okumura et al., 2013a, 2013b) because infants attribute communicative intentions to humans, but not to robot agents (Boyer, Pan & Bertenthal, 2011; Okumura, Kanakogi, Kanda, Ishiguro & Itakura, 2013)."

The dissociation this paper reports for beeps was already reported for robots: infants followed the robot's gaze, and learned nothing from it.

ostensive-signalling proposed that the interesting direction for emotion-engine might be the outbound one — the robot producing ostensive signals so a person knows they are being addressed. This is the first evidence in the wiki bearing on that, and it is negative. It does not close the question: the robot was a humanoid (Kanda & Ishiguro's lineage), the subjects were infants, and a function-orientedness argument would predict a dog-like signal to work where a human-imitating one fails — which is testable and would be a genuine contribution. But the proposal can no longer be made as though nothing were known.

Second-hand and unheld. Do not cite Okumura et al. (2013a/b) or Boyer et al. (2011) from this page. Logged in citation-backlog as a priority acquisition.

Limitations

Stated by the authors, and they matter:

  • Video, not live. "Since our findings were just based on video stimuli, we do not know how much they can be generalized in daily life." Real caregivers compound multiple ostensive cues simultaneously and sequentially, and exaggerate their demonstrations dynamically (Fukuyama et al. 2015).
  • Direct gaze is present in every condition but No cue. It was added deliberately, to keep the infants looking at the face so the two critical cues could work; effects are measured relative to the Gaze-only baseline. The authors argue the between-condition differences are therefore attributable to the cues. But it means eye contact is never isolated as an ostensive cue in this study.
  • The mechanism is left open: whether infants are predisposed to treat ostensive cues as privileged, or learn that they indicate communicative intent. "There is probably some truth to both accounts."
  • Between-subjects, 20 per cell, single age (9 months).
  • IDS is the only ostensive cue tested. Nothing here licenses claims about name-calling, pointing or gaze alternation.

A tension with the dog literature, not resolved

Eye contact: strongest cue in dogs, insufficient on its own here

topal-2014-ostensive states flatly that eye contact is the strongest ostensive signal for dogs and that dog-directed speech is "secondary but important". In this study the ordering looks inverted: direct gaze alone (Gaze-only) produced gaze-following but no object processing and no object preference, while IDS produced both. The authors conclude "the effect of a direct gaze cue alone may only weakly enhance object learning".

Both positions, and why they may not actually conflict. Different species; different measures (dog studies score choice behaviour in referential tasks, this one scores encoding and preference); and critically, this was video — a pre-recorded face cannot make contingent eye contact, which is precisely the property emotion-attribution identifies as doing the work (ortony-2022-cognitive-structure p. 180). A non-contingent recorded gaze is a weak test of eye contact as an ostensive signal.

Better supported for which claim: Topál for dogs, on a wider evidence base but in a review chapter reporting others' work; Okumura for infants, on direct experimental data but with a stimulus limitation the authors name. Neither settles what a robot should emit. Do not let the wiki quietly assume eye contact is the cue to build first — see Q21.
