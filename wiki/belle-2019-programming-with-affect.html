Shakir Belle, Curtis Gittens (University of the West Indies, Barbados) and T.C. Nicholas Graham (Queen's University, Kingston), Programming with Affect: How Behaviour Trees and a Lightweight Cognitive Architecture Enable the Development of Non-Player Characters with Emotions, IEEE GEM 2019, pp. 1–8.

Reference [9]/[10] of research-proposal, now held — the last unheld numeric reference, and the one flagged as most important after colledanchise-ogren-behavior-trees turned out to contain no emotion content.

What it is

EMoBeT (Emotion Modelling Behaviour Tree): a framework that makes mood a first-class property of a behaviour tree, by adding two node types.

New node Function
Emotion Adder Triggers an emotion in the character. Single child. The developer supplies the possible emotions (from the OCC set) and the triggers that fire them
E-Selector Selects an action based on current mood. Exactly two children, labelled positive and negative; picks the one matching the sign of the character's mood octant

The stated motivation is that existing tools "do not treat mood as a first-class property: specifically, they do not provide mechanisms for modifying an NPC's mood, or for allowing the NPC's current mood to influence its behaviour."

The architecture

Built on ALMA (Gebhard's A Layered Model of Affect), reimplemented light-weight so it runs in real time on ordinary gaming hardware. Four subsystems:

  • Personality — the Five Factor Model (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism). Sets the character's default mood and scales emotion intensity: "having a highly neurotic personality will cause the intensities of negative emotions to be higher".
  • Emotion — short-term states from OCC (Ortony, Clore & Collins). Each carries type, intensity and trigger. The OCC primary is now held — ortony-2022-cognitive-structure, occ-model — but note Belle et al. cite the 1988 edition, and the 2022 edition revises the emotion set from 22 to 24 types and replaces "eliciting" with "emergence" conditions. Their emotion set should not be assumed identical to the current model's.
  • Mood — mid-term, and "the deciding factor for all NPC decisions". Represented in PAD space (Pleasure, Arousal, Dominance, each −1…1), giving eight octants: Exuberant, Dependent, Relaxed, Docile, Hostile, Anxious, Disdainful, Bored. Intensity is slight, moderate or full by distance from the origin.
  • Memory — long- and short-term, decaying on the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve. On re-encountering a player the character recalls its strongest memory of them and re-triggers the associated emotions.

Mood updates via an emotion centre — the combined PAD point of all felt emotions. If the emotion centre lies between the current mood and the origin, mood is pushed further into its current octant; otherwise mood moves toward the emotion centre.

Three design requirements are stated: influence (emotion must affect decisions), persistence (emotions do not vanish after the event), and interface (use behaviour trees, because developers already know them).

Why this matters for the thesis

The nearest prior art to the decision layer — and it stops where the rest of the corpus stops

This is the only source in the corpus that joins emotion to behaviour trees, and it genuinely pre-empts one thing: behaviour-tree nodes that make affective state first-class in action selection. The thesis cannot claim novelty for that idea in the abstract.

But look at what triggers the emotions. Every emotion in every scenario is fired by a scripted player dialogue choice — the developer enumerates the options and hard-wires which emotion each one adds. That is hand-labelled discrete events, exactly as in szabo-2011-emotional-engine's MATLAB GUI.

Two entirely independent traditions — Hungarian ethorobotics and Caribbean / Canadian game AI — arrive at the same architecture and stop at the same place. Neither closes the perception-to-emotion link. That convergence strengthens the claim in thesis considerably: the gap is not an oversight of one research network, it is where the field as a whole stops. (Synthesis drawn here; neither source cites the other.)

What EMoBeT does not have, and the thesis therefore still owns: no robot, no sensing of any kind, no learning, no internal state of the agent, no real environment — and no evaluation (below).

The E-Selector answers a live design question — differently from the wiki

behaviour-trees has an open item: does emotional state gate the tree, re-weight it, or parameterise leaf actions?

Belle et al. choose gating, and in its crudest form: the E-Selector takes exactly two children and picks by the sign of the mood octant. An eight-octant PAD mood is collapsed to one bit at the point of decision.

That is worth recording as prior art, and worth differing from. The wiki's existing argument — that parameterising leaf actions best fits dog-human-interaction-model, where the same primitive approach appears under joy, fear, neutral and anger distinguished by manner rather than kind — now has a concrete alternative to be defended against. It is also a much better fit for robot-platform, where expression is continuous (LED colour, speed, proximity) rather than a choice between two scripted lines.

Comparison with the corpus' own engine

szabo-2011-emotional-engine EMoBeT
Temporal layers short / medium / long-term emotion / mood / personality
Emotion model Panksepp circuits + dog ethology OCC
Mood space 5 discrete states PAD, 8 octants
Long-term layer temperament (dropped in the 2012 paper) Five Factor personality, retained
Arbitration hierarchical rule tree, 5 states E-Selector, binary
Memory of individuals none yes, with Ebbinghaus decay
Input hand-labelled events (GUI) hand-scripted dialogue choices
Validation wizard-of-oz human studies none

The three-layer temporal structure is a genuine convergence between the two, and neither cites the other. EMoBeT's episodic memory of specific individuals is something the Hungarian lineage lacks entirely, and is directly relevant to social-competence and to Q16 — a robot that remembers a particular person across sessions is what a longitudinal study would need.

Method and limitations

There is no evaluation, despite the section title

§V is headed "Scenarios and Evaluation", but contains only worked traces: three scripted dialogue scenarios (father and son after a basketball game; a lunch-choice conversation) with tables of PAD values changing as emotions fire. No users, no participants, no performance or timing measurements, no comparison condition.

The conclusion defers all of it: "For our future work, we will build richer scenarios in order to provide a better testbed... We will also implement it in the game... Finally, we will test the usability and performance of the system to determine if it is a viable approach."

So the nearest prior art to this thesis' decision layer is a demonstration paper whose authors state they have not yet established that the approach is viable. That is a low bar to clear, and the thesis should say so accurately and without overclaiming — the idea is prior art, the evidence is not.

Further limitations:

  • 8 pages, conference paper, IEEE GEM — a games/media venue, peer-reviewed but not a robotics or HRI venue.
  • Thoroughly anthropomorphic. OCC, PAD, Five Factor Model, dialogue trees, human social scenarios. This is precisely the affective-computing tradition that ethorobotics rejects — see Q5 in open-questions.
  • Real-time claim is asserted, not measured: "can be computed in real-time on standard gaming computers" with no benchmark.
  • The related-work survey is useful and covers ground the wiki lacks: affective gaming, GAMYGDALA (an OCC-based "black box appraisal" engine), and cognitive architectures (SOAR, ACT-R, SHERCA). Also Johansson & Dell'Acqua's earlier emotional selector node, which sorts children by planning effort, risk and time — which Belle et al. criticise as "based on an intuitive, instead of psychological approach".

A design stance worth quoting, because the thesis will face the same choice: their approach "focuses on simulating human-like emotions and having these emotions effect the decision-making process regardless of whether it is an optimal outcome for the NPC", explicitly against approaches that seek optimal responses. Under rl-for-behaviour-learning this thesis is doing the opposite — optimising — and that tension needs an answer.
