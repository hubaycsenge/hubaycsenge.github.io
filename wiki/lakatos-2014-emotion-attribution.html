The companion empirical study to gacsi-2016-emotion-attribution, and the more directly relevant of the two: live interaction rather than video, and it tests a secondary emotion (guilt), not just primary ones.

Design

Robot: MogiRobi (BME Mechatronics + ELTE Ethology) — mobile, with a 3-DOF head, 2-DOF neck, 1-DOF ear-like appendices and a 2-DOF rear antenna. The antenna and appendices deliberately bear little physical resemblance to a dog's tail and ears; they are functional analogues, not imitations. See prior-robot-platforms.

Both experiments were wizard-of-oz — teleoperated from an adjoining room, participants unaware. Family Dog Project lab, ELTE. Four cameras, Solomon Coder for behavioural coding, non-parametric statistics throughout. Ethics approval from the ELTE Institute of Biology IRB.

Experiment 1 (N = 48 analysed of 71) — play with two balls. The robot showed canine 'happy' behaviour (raised antenna/appendices, antenna wagging, approaching, retrieving) toward a 'preferred' ball and canine 'fearful' behaviour (lowered antenna and appendices, backing, maximising distance) toward a 'non-preferred' ball, counterbalanced by colour and order.

Experiment 2 — guilt attribution: could participants tell from the robot's greeting behaviour whether it had transgressed a rule?

Results

  • Happiness recognised 83.3% in forced choice; fear only 47.9% — about as many participants read the robot as indifferent or emotionless as read it as fearful.
  • Compare Kismet (human-like face): joy 82.4%, fear 47.1%. Probo: joy 100%, fear 65%. The dog-derived body-movement approach matched a humanoid face without needing one. See emotion-recognition-benchmarks.
  • Participants played more (or exclusively) with the 'preferred' ball, and explained their choice by reference to the robot's inner states.
  • Experiment 2: participants could detect transgression from greeting behaviour — a secondary emotion read successfully.
  • NARS (Negative Attitudes towards Robots Scale) dropped after interaction (dog owners 2.00 → 1.83; non-owners 2.83 → 2.08). Brief interaction improved attitudes.

Limitations the authors state

  • The ball preference may be explained by the robot's inactivity toward the non-preferred ball rather than by attributed emotion. The confound is acknowledged, not resolved.
  • Participants often read 'fear' as indifference or reluctance, and responded with anger or embarrassment — discouraging the robot, negative comments, hands on hips. A mis-read negative emotion produced a negative user reaction.
  • Wizard-of-oz: nothing here tests an autonomous system. The engine this thesis builds is precisely what was faked in these studies — see emotion-engine.
  • Fear recognition being poor is consistent with dog owners also being bad at recognising fear in real dogs from visual signals alone (Wan et al.).

Why this matters for the thesis

It supplies the evaluation template for validation-study: wizard-of-oz baseline, pre/post NARS, open-ended before forced-choice, behavioural coding alongside self-report, and a counterbalanced within-subject design. It also sets the bar: any autonomous engine should reach ~83% on a positive state and must address the fear/indifference confusion.
