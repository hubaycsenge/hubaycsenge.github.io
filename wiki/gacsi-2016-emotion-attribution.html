The strongest empirical support in the corpus for the thesis premise that emotion-attribution to a non-humanoid robot works. A candidate for [2] of research-proposal. #needs-source

Design

  • Robot: PeopleBot (MobileRobots Inc.) on a P3-DX differential-drive base — chest-level touchscreen "head" that cannot move independently, no face, no eyes, no verbal ability, one 5-DOF arm plus a mock-up arm. Deliberately unlike both humans and dogs. Industrial PC, Ubuntu + Xenomai, Player/Stage.
  • Dog: 3-year-old male Belgian malinois, cued by trainer.
  • Stimuli: 5 short video clips each (8–14 s) for joy, fear, anger, sadness, neutral, recorded in a neutral lab so social context could not aid attribution.
  • N = 81 (78 for the open-ended part), aged 18–26, 39 dog owners. Blind to purpose; open-ended questionnaire first, then forced choice, to avoid priming.

Behaviour derivation is the methodologically important part: dog emotional behaviours were extracted from a meta-analysis of prior ethological studies in named contexts (joy = greeting the owner / going for a walk; anger = facing a threatening stranger; fear = facing a threatening stranger; sadness = separation / being scolded; neutral = 'mobile owner' task), then re-expressed within the robot's embodiment — not copied. See dog-human-interaction-model.

Robot vocalisations were synthesised to follow Morton's rules (as dog barks do): aggressive = low-pitched, noisy, fast-pulsing; joyful = raised pitch, less noisy, fast-pulsing; sad = elongated, slow-pulsing, decreasing pitch. Fear and neutral had no sound, because dogs rarely vocalise in those contexts.

Results

  • Participants spontaneously attributed emotions to the robot (12–57% by emotion) and the dog (31–68%) with no prompting about emotion.
  • Forced choice: all dog and all robot clips identified above chance (all p < 0.001). Mean success — dog 0.78, robot 0.75.
  • No effect of experience with dogs (p = 0.267) — notable, because it means the model does not require the user to be dog-literate.
  • No overall dog-vs-robot difference (p = 0.16), but an order effect: seeing dog videos first improved robot recognition (p = 0.002). Among robot-first participants only, dogs were still recognised better (p = 0.025).
  • Anger was sometimes read as joy, never the reverse. Robot 'sadness' → 'fear'; robot 'fear' → 'neutral'.

Benchmark context (facial-expression robots, from the paper): PeopleBot 75%, Probo 73%, Kismet 73%, Eddie 57%, Feelix 45%. Collected in emotion-recognition-benchmarks.

Notable methodological stance

"From the viewpoint of robot development, participants' impressions by definition cannot be false. Their subjective evaluation can best validate the robot's expressive behaviours."

Attribution of no emotion, or of the "wrong" emotion, means the robot's actions were badly set — not that the participant erred. Directly relevant to designing validation-study.

Limitations

  • Neutral lab, no social context, no interaction — the authors say results should not be extrapolated to real life, where context strongly aids attribution. This is exactly the gap the thesis claims to address (real-time, real environments — see emotion-engine).
  • Video clips, not live interaction. Robot teleoperated.
  • Narrow sample (university students, 18–26).
  • Neither the dog nor the robot could fully approach the viewer, which the authors believe depressed recognition rates.

Tension with the thesis premise

The introduction cites Petisca, Dias & Paiva (2014) that in some cases robot emotion expression leads to poorer perception by humans. research-proposal assumes affect straightforwardly "helps cooperation and acceptance". The condition under which it helps is not established. See Q8 in open-questions.
