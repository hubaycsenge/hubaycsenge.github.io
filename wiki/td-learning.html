The central algorithmic idea of sutton-barto-2018-rl — the authors call Ch. 6 "the most important for the subject and for the rest of the book" — and the point where the book makes contact with animal behaviour and with the brain.

The TD error

For state values, the TD error is

δ_t = R_{t+1} + γ·V(S_{t+1}) − V(S_t)

the discrepancy between an earlier and a later estimate of expected future reward. Learning updates parameters in proportion to δ and to an eligibility trace.

Two distinctions the book insists on, both relevant to this wiki:

  • Reward signal vs reinforcement signal. The reward signal R defines the problem; the reinforcement signal is "the major factor directing changes a learning algorithm makes in an agent's policy, value estimates, or environment models" — for TD methods that is δ, not R (§15.2, §14 terminology). Inside δ, R_{t+1} is the primary reinforcement contribution and γV(S_{t+1}) − V(S_t) is the conditioned reinforcement contribution — the formal counterpart of the secondary reinforcer in conditioning.
  • Reward vs value. Reward is immediate desirability, value is long-run desirability. "Rewards are in a sense primary, whereas values, as predictions of rewards, are secondary... it is much harder to determine values than it is to determine rewards" (§1.3).

The TD model of classical conditioning

The TD algorithm doubles as a model of animal learning (§14.2.3). It generalises the Rescorla–Wagner model by moving from trial level to real time, t now indexing time steps of the order of 10 ms rather than whole trials. Two things follow that Rescorla–Wagner cannot produce:

  • Higher-order conditioning falls out of bootstrapping — since V(S_{t+1}) − V(S_t) appears in δ alongside R_{t+1}, "as far as learning is concerned there is no difference between a temporal difference and the occurrence of a US" (§14.2.3).
  • Timing effects within a trial — dependence of learning on the interstimulus interval, anticipatory conditioned responses, and the prediction that temporal primacy overrides blocking: a newly added CS whose onset precedes the pretrained CS is not blocked, and the pretrained CS loses associative strength. This was a prediction of the model before it was observed; Kehoe, Schreurs & Graham (1987) later confirmed it in the rabbit nictitating membrane preparation, and noted that non-TD models have difficulty explaining the data (§14.2.4).

Stimulus representation matters more than the algorithm. Three representations are compared (§14.2.4): presence (one feature per CS while it is present), complete serial compound (CSC — a tapped delay line, a precise internal clock), and microstimulus (overlapping internal signals that broaden and weaken with elapsed time). The authors call the CSC "a useful fiction", unrealistic as a hypothesis about the brain, and say the common view that it is an essential part of the TD model "is mistaken". Which one is used determines whether the model reproduces the shape and timing of the conditioned response at all.

Why this matters for emotion-engine

szabo-2011-emotional-engine hand-specifies peak widths, decline rates and saturation levels per emotion — that is a hand-authored temporal profile. The TD model's lesson is that such profiles are largely determined by the stimulus representation rather than by the learning rule. If any part of the engine's dynamics is to be learnt rather than tuned, the representation is where the design effort goes. (Reading applied to this wiki, not a claim of the book.)

The reward prediction error hypothesis

The best-known link between RL and neuroscience (§15.3–15.6). First stated by Montague, Dayan & Sejnowski (1996) on the basis of experiments from Wolfram Schultz's laboratory in the late 1980s and 1990s.

The hypothesis: the phasic activity of dopamine-producing neurons signals a reward prediction error — specifically a TD error — rather than reward itself. Four observed features of dopamine neuron activity that δ reproduces:

  1. Phasic response occurs only when a rewarding event is unpredicted.
  2. Early in learning, neutral cues preceding reward produce no response; with learning they acquire one.
  3. When an earlier cue reliably precedes an already-predictive cue, the response shifts to the earlier cue and ceases for the later one.
  4. If a predicted reward is omitted, activity drops below baseline shortly after the expected time — without any external cue marking that time.

The authors are candid about the fit. It is not perfect: when reward arrives earlier than expected, dopamine neurons do not show the negative excursion at the original expected time that the TD model with a CSC representation predicts (Hollerman & Schultz 1998). Several repairs have been proposed — modified CSC, microstimulus representations, longer eligibility traces — and "many fine details of TD-error behavior depend on subtle interactions between eligibility traces, discounting, and stimulus representations" (§15.6).

They also stress that the correspondence was unplanned: TD learning and its connection to dynamic programming were developed years before the dopamine experiments, "in total absence of any knowledge about the relevant properties of dopamine neurons".

Contradiction — Panksepp rejects the reduction

The RPE hypothesis is about dopamine and reward prediction. This book never mentions Panksepp, and makes no claim about any named affective system.

The Panksepp primaries, now held, reject the bridge explicitly. §7 of panksepp-2005-affective-consciousness is titled "The SEEKING/expectancy/wanting system of the brain: It's not just 'reward prediction error'"; panksepp-2011-emotional-circuits calls the restriction "a fundamental error... mixing levels of analysis".

Not a symmetrical dispute. Panksepp accepts that SEEKING is dopaminergic and does not contest Schultz's data; he contests that RPE exhausts the system. Sutton & Barto describe a learning signal and never discuss affect. Better supported for "what does phasic dopamine compute": the RPE hypothesis. Better supported for "what is SEEKING for": Panksepp, unopposed.

Full record on panksepp-affective-systems.

Two further caveats the authors give (§15.4, §15.7): dopamine's role in punishment "remains controversial", it "can function differently in non-mammals", and the dopamine signal is explicitly not the master reward signal R — "one should not necessarily be able to probe the brain and record any signal like Rt in the activity of any single neuron".

Eligibility traces

Decaying traces marking which parameters are eligible for modification when a reinforcement signal arrives (§14.4, §15.8). Two kinds, and the distinction is the whole difference between the actor and the critic in actor-critic:

  • Non-contingent — depend only on presynaptic (input) activity. Adequate for learning to predict.
  • Contingent — depend additionally on the unit's own output. Necessary for learning to control, because they record which action was taken in which state.

Klopf's (1972, 1982) "hedonistic neuron" hypothesis is the origin of the idea: individual neurons adjust their synapses on the basis of the rewarding or punishing consequences of their own action potentials, with synaptically-local traces marking eligibility. The book's exponentially decaying traces are described as "placeholders" for Klopf's richer original conception, in which the shape of a trace is "like a histogram of the durations of the feedback loops in which the neuron is embedded" (§15.9).

The neuroscience counterpart is reward-modulated spike-timing-dependent plasticity — regular STDP changes occur only if a neuromodulatory pulse arrives within a time window afterwards. Note the unresolved discrepancy in the book about how long that window is, recorded on sutton-barto-2018-rl.
