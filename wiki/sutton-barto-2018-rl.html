Richard S. Sutton and Andrew G. Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, second edition. MIT Press, 2018. The standard textbook of the field.

Scope of this ingest, and provenance

Read closely: Ch. 14 (Psychology), Ch. 15 (Neuroscience), §17.4 (Designing Reward Signals), §17.5, plus §1.3–1.4, §3.1–3.2 and the Comments on Terminology at the end of Ch. 14. Parts I and II (the algorithms themselves, Ch. 2–13) are recorded structurally and read only where Ch. 14/15 depend on them. Cite this page by section number, not page number — the PDF in raw/ was printed from a browser (cairo, 2026-07-27), so it is one of the freely circulated electronic versions and its pagination may not match the printed MIT Press edition.

What this book is for this wiki

It is not a source on emotion. Verified against the full text: Panksepp does not appear; emotion appears three times, all incidental; affective appears twice in the body, both times meaning "the ventral striatum assigns affective value to sensations". §14.7 says explicitly that the book omits links "to ecological and evolutionary aspects of behavior studied by ethologists and behavioral ecologists".

What it is: the formal apparatus for the animal-learning phenomena that the rest of this corpus models by hand. Ch. 14 covers conditioning, the Law of Effect, shaping, motivation, cognitive maps and habit formation; Ch. 15 covers the neural substrate. That is the same subject matter as dog-human-interaction-model and fuzzy-automaton-fri, approached from computation instead of ethology.

Given the decision to use RL to teach robot-platform behaviour patterns, the design argument built on this source is in rl-for-behaviour-learning.

Takeaways

  • Prediction ↔ classical conditioning; control ↔ instrumental conditioning. The book's two algorithm families map onto psychology's two conditioning paradigms, a correspondence the authors call "a convenient first approximation" rather than an identity (§14.1). See conditioning.
  • An animal's instinctive repertoire is the admissible action set. Thorndike's cats selected from their "instinctual impulses"; Sutton & Barto map this onto A(s) and note that "specifying these sets is an important aspect of reinforcement learning because it can radically simplify learning" (§14.3). This is the single most consequential sentence in the book for this thesis — see rl-for-behaviour-learning.
  • Reward is internal to the agent, and the agent is not the robot. Rt is "more like a reward signal in neuroscience, which is a signal internal to the brain" (§14 terminology). The agent–environment boundary "is typically not the same as the physical boundary of a robot's or animal's body" — motors, sensors and the reward computation itself sit on the environment side (§3.1).
  • Reward ≠ reinforcement. The reward signal defines the problem; the reinforcement signal drives parameter updates and is usually the TD error δ = R + γV(S′) − V(S), not R (§15.2, §14 terminology). See td-learning.
  • Phasic dopamine activity behaves like a TD error, not like reward — the reward prediction error hypothesis (§15.3–15.6). See td-learning.
  • Actor–critic plausibly maps onto the striatum: critic in ventral, actor in dorsal, dopamine broadcasting the same scalar δ to both (§15.7). See actor-critic.
  • Model-free ↔ habitual, model-based ↔ goal-directed, with a documented shift from goal-directed to habitual under overtraining (§14.6). See habitual-vs-goal-directed.
  • Reward design is the hard part, and the book offers warnings rather than recipes (§17.4). See reward-design.
  • Online, incremental learning on real systems is named as unsolved (§17.5). This supports rather than undermines the thesis' framing of real-time real-environment operation as hard.

Method

A textbook, not a study — it reports no new experiments. Its evidential structure is worth stating because it affects how it can be cited:

  • Ch. 1–13 are mathematics and algorithms, with simulation results.
  • Ch. 14–15 are literature reviews of animal-learning and neuroscience experiments performed by others (Pavlov, Thorndike, Rescorla & Wagner, Kamin, Adams & Dickinson, Tolman, Schultz's group, Yagishita et al.), read through the RL formalism. Claims here are second-hand; the primary sources are listed in citation-backlog.
  • Ch. 16 is applications and case studies; Ch. 17 is the authors' assessment of open problems.

Peer review: MIT Press monograph, not a peer-reviewed article. Ch. 14 and 15 were read in draft by named domain experts (Moore, Botvinick, Daw, Dayan, Niv), which the authors state in the preface — a reasonable but weaker guarantee than review.

Limitations, for this thesis specifically

  • No emotion content and no ethology content. Nothing here licenses a claim about affect. In particular it cannot be used to support the Panksepp–dopamine bridge; see the warning on panksepp-affective-systems.
  • Trial-structured laboratory paradigms. The animal experiments reviewed are rats, pigeons, rabbits and monkeys in controlled apparatus. Transfer to a mobile robot in a corridor with an untrained human is not addressed.
  • The authors' own assessment of online learning is pessimistic (§17.5): current deep methods "struggle to learn rapidly in the incremental, online settings that are most natural for the reinforcement learning algorithms emphasized in this book"; catastrophic interference is unsolved; "an honest assessment has to be that current deep learning methods are not well suited to online learning". They also name safe embedding of RL agents in physical environments as "one of the most pressing areas for future research".
  • Scalar reward. The authors themselves flag this as at odds with the fact that "animals' appetitive and aversive systems have qualitatively different properties and involve different brain mechanisms", and say a separate appetitive/aversive treatment is a direction they pass over (§14 terminology). For a multi-dimensional emotional state this is a real mismatch — see reward-design.
  • Single-agent. Multi-agent RL is out of scope except for the team argument in §15.10, so the book says nothing about the interaction as a two-party process, which is what interspecific-hri insists on.

Inconsistency inside this source

The eligibility window for reward-modulated STDP is given as lasting "up to 10 seconds" in §15.8 and again in the §15.13 summary, both citing Yagishita et al. (2014). The Bibliographical and Historical Remarks for §15.8 report the same paper as finding a window "of from 0.3 to 2 seconds after STDP stimulation". Two orders of magnitude apart, unresolvable from this book. If the number matters, read Yagishita et al. (2014) directly — logged in citation-backlog. Nothing in this wiki currently depends on it.

Structure, for navigation

  • Part I — Tabular (Ch. 2–8): bandits, finite MDPs, dynamic programming, Monte Carlo, temporal-difference learning, n-step bootstrapping, planning (Dyna, prioritised sweeping, RTDP, MCTS).
  • Part II — Approximate (Ch. 9–13): on- and off-policy prediction and control with function approximation, eligibility traces, policy gradient (REINFORCE, actor–critic).
  • Part III — Looking Deeper (Ch. 14–17): Psychology, Neuroscience, applications and case studies, frontiers.

The authors' own reading advice (preface): Ch. 1, Ch. 2 through §2.4, Ch. 3, then select; "Chapter 6 is the most important for the subject and for the rest of the book"; a planning-focused course should cover Ch. 8.
