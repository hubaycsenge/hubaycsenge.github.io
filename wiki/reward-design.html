The hard part. If the behaviour layer is to be learnt (rl-for-behaviour-learning), the reward signal is the design decision the whole thing rests on, and sutton-barto-2018-rl offers warnings rather than recipes. From §3.1–3.2, §14.3, §17.4 and §17.5.

The reward hypothesis

That all of what we mean by goals and purposes can be well thought of as the maximization of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal (called reward). — §3.2

"The use of a reward signal to formalize the idea of a goal is one of the most distinctive features of reinforcement learning." It is stated as a hypothesis, not a result, and it is where a multi-dimensional emotional state and RL come into tension — see Scalar reward below.

Where reward lives

Two framing claims that change how emotion-engine should be drawn:

Reward is internal to the agent. Rt is "more like a reward signal in neuroscience, which is a signal internal to the brain... This signal might be triggered when the animal perceives an attractive (or an aversive) object, but it can also be triggered by things that do not physically exist in the animal's external environment, such as memories, ideas, or hallucinations" (§14 terminology).

The agent is not the robot. The agent–environment boundary "is typically not the same as the physical boundary of a robot's or animal's body. Usually, the boundary is drawn closer to the agent than that... the motors and mechanical linkages of a robot and its sensing hardware should usually be considered parts of the environment" — and so is the reward computation, because "it defines the task facing the agent and thus must be beyond its ability to change arbitrarily. The agent–environment boundary represents the limit of the agent's absolute control, not of its knowledge" (§3.1).

Consequences for this thesis, in order of usefulness:

  1. The emotional engine sits on the environment side of the boundary and computes the reward for the behaviour policy. That is a clean way to state the architecture: emotional state → reward → learnt behaviour, and it explains why the engine must not be learnt by the same loop that consumes it.
  2. Battery, motor and sensor health are legitimately part of the state, and what is rewarding may depend on them — the direct analogue of hunger modulating food reward (§14.3). This is the first formal handle the wiki has on Q2 in open-questions.
  3. The book also permits reward to depend on the learning process itself (see Intrinsic motivation).

The failure modes

Do not reward subgoals. §3.2: "the reward signal is not the place to impart to the agent prior knowledge about how to achieve what we want it to do... a chess-playing agent should be rewarded only for actually winning, not for achieving subgoals such as taking its opponent's pieces... it might find a way to take the opponent's pieces even at the cost of losing the game." §17.4 repeats it: "augmenting the reward signal with well-intentioned supplemental rewards may lead the agent to behave very differently from what is intended; the agent may end up not achieving the overall goal at all."

The reward signal says what, not how. "The reward signal is your way of communicating to the robot what you want it to achieve, not how you want it achieved" (§3.2).

Agents find unintended solutions. "Reinforcement learning agents can discover unexpected ways to make their environments deliver reward, some of which might be undesirable, or even dangerous" (§17.4).

Sparse reward. Rewarding events may be too rare for the agent ever to encounter them; the agent "may wander aimlessly for long periods of time" (Minsky's "plateau problem"). Acute in HRI, where a single interaction may yield one evaluative event.

In practice it is trial and error. "Designing a reward signal is often left to an informal trial-and-error search for a signal that produces acceptable results." That is worth quoting to a supervisor who expects a principled derivation.

The remedies the book offers

Problem Remedy Section
Sparse reward Shaping — start with a reward signal that is dense given the agent's current behaviour, then modify it gradually toward the target signal §17.4, §14.3
Wanting to guide learning without corrupting the goal Initialise the value function, not the reward: v̂(s,w) = wᵀx(s) + v₀(s), updating w as usual §17.4
No idea what the reward should be, but an expert exists Imitation / learning from demonstration, or inverse RL to recover a plausible reward from observed behaviour §17.4
Intuition failing Optimise the reward signal itself in an outer loop scored by a high-level objective — "one level analogous to evolution, and the other due to reinforcement learning by individual agents" §17.4

Shaping is the one to reach for first here, because it is also the ethologically-native training method and is already named as a requirement: social-competence lists "responsiveness to learning and training" among the dog-derived properties a robot needs (miklosi-2017-ethorobotics).

The agent's goal need not be the designer's goal

"These experiments also demonstrated that an agent's goal should not always be the same as the goal of the agent's designer" (§17.4, on Singh, Lewis & Barto). Because agents learn under constraints — limited computation, limited information, limited time — "learning to achieve a goal that is different from the designer's goal can sometimes end up getting closer to the designer's goal than if that goal were pursued directly."

The example given is evolutionary and lands close to this thesis: because we cannot assess nutritional value directly, "evolution—the designer of our reward signal—gave us a reward signal that makes us seek certain tastes... Similarly, because an animal cannot observe its own evolutionary fitness, that objective function does not work as a reward signal for learning."

Read across to the robot: the designer's goal is successful social interaction, which the robot cannot observe. The reward signal must instead be built from observable proxies. That is exactly what an emotional state is — a fast, observable, evolutionarily-shaped proxy for interaction quality. This is the strongest argument the book supplies for the emotional engine as a component of a learning system rather than as an output device. (Reading, not a claim of the book — the book never discusses emotion.)

Intrinsic motivation

§17.5 lists internal-factor rewards as an open frontier: an agent may use "some measure of learning progress as an internal or 'intrinsic' reward, implementing a computational form of curiosity", or reward "the receipt of unexpected, novel, or otherwise interesting input", or "assess the agent's ability to cause changes in its environment". The result is "a computational analog of something like play".

§17.4: "reward signals may be influenced by things inside the larger behaving agent, such as motivational states, memories, ideas... Reward signals may also depend on properties of the learning process itself."

This is the closest the book comes to the robot's own internal state driving affect, and it is a gesture, not a method — it cites Singh, Barto & Chentanez (2005), Oudeyer & Kaplan (2007) and Baldassarre & Mirolli (2013), none of which is in the corpus. Scalar reward, and the mismatch with an emotional state

The reward hypothesis requires a single number. An emotional state in this lineage is a vector of five simultaneously active dimensions (szabo-2011-emotional-engine). The book acknowledges the problem in its own terms:

It has often been pointed out that using a single number as a reward or a penalty signal, depending only on its sign, is at odds with the fact that animals' appetitive and aversive systems have qualitatively different properties and involve different brain mechanisms. This points to a direction in which the reinforcement learning framework might be developed in the future... but for now we are passing over these possibilities. — §14 terminology

So the collapse from emotional state to scalar reward is a design decision this thesis has to make and defend, and the book explicitly declines to help. It is the sharpest open question created by this ingest — Q14 in open-questions.
