People attribute emotions to artificial agents, and this facilitates interaction research-proposal. Now the best-evidenced claim in the wiki — two studies in the corpus test it directly.

The evidence

Spontaneous, unprompted attribution. In gacsi-2016-emotion-attribution, participants were given no hint that the study was about emotion and were asked only "write down briefly what is happening on each video". They spontaneously credited the robot with emotional states in 12–57% of cases depending on the emotion (dog: 31–68%), and gave reliably higher scores to emotionally loaded than to neutral clips.

Accurate attribution. All robot clips identified above chance; 75% mean success against the live dog's 78%, with no significant overall difference. Kismet-comparable rates without a face. See emotion-recognition-benchmarks.

Behavioural consequences, not just reports. In lakatos-2014-emotion-attribution, participants played more (or exclusively) with the ball the robot had expressed "happiness" toward, and explained their choice by reference to the robot's inner states. Attribution changed what people did.

Attitudes improved. NARS scores dropped after interaction, for dog owners (2.00 → 1.83) and non-owners alike (2.83 → 2.08).

Independent of expertise. No effect of dog ownership on emotion recognition in either study. The model does not require a dog-literate user.

Secondary emotions too. Participants could detect from greeting behaviour that the robot had transgressed a rule — guilt attribution, not merely primary affect.

The counter-evidence

Attribution is not automatically beneficial

gacsi-2016-emotion-attribution cites Petisca, Dias & Paiva (2014): in some cases a robot's emotion expression leads to poorer perception by humans. The paper is not in the corpus — obtain it (citation-backlog).

lakatos-2014-emotion-attribution observed the mechanism directly: participants often read the robot's "fear" as indifference or reluctance and responded with anger and embarrassment — discouraging the robot, making negative comments, hands on hips. A misread negative emotion produced a hostile user reaction rather than a sympathetic one.

So the premise in step 2 of thesis should be qualified: attribution helps when the expressed emotion is correctly read. Since fear is reliably misread across every robot measured, this is not a marginal caveat. Q8 in open-questions.

Design implication

gacsi-2016-emotion-attribution takes an unusual and useful stance: participant impressions "by definition cannot be false" — attributing no emotion or the wrong emotion means the robot's actions were badly set, not that the user erred. That reframes evaluation as a design signal and should shape validation-study.

Open

  • Under what conditions attribution helps versus misleads — the central unresolved question above.
  • Whether attribution depends on morphology, behavioural contingency, or both. Indirect evidence favours contingency: the same design worked across four very different embodiments (lakatos-2014-embodiments-demo).
  • Over-attribution risk: expectations the robot cannot meet. Not studied in the corpus. #needs-source

An independent criterion, from cognitive psychology — 2026-07-28

ortony-2022-cognitive-structure reaches this concept from outside HRI entirely, and supplies something the ethology literature has not: a stated criterion for when it happens.

OCC's Attraction emotions split on whether the object is implicitly viewed by the experiencer as being capable or incapable of experiencing emotions (Table 8.1, p. 180). Emotion-capable objects afford "Affection" and "Enmity"; emotion-incapable ones afford only "Appreciation" and "Distaste". The authors name the exception explicitly: people "sometimes personify certain kinds of inanimate objects such as houseplants and domestic robots".

The criterion given is not animacy and not human-likeness:

"Affection" emotions "are contingent on a view of the object, however misguided, as being somehow capable of at least the possibility of some reciprocity of feeling or concern." (p. 180)

And the worked example is a dog against a pet skunk. The dog qualifies because it is "highly responsive to others", exchanges gaze, and responds "to even slight movements on [its] owner's part"; the skunk fails because it is "remarkably oblivious to humans, never exchanging gazes".

Attribution is not a feeling humans have about a robot — it is a category assignment, and it has a testable trigger

The corpus has established that people attribute emotions to robots (gacsi-2016-emotion-attribution, lakatos-2014-emotion-attribution) and that the effect survives across embodiments (lakatos-2014-embodiments-demo). It has not said what causes the assignment. OCC proposes: apparent responsiveness and possible reciprocity, evidenced by gaze exchange and contingent reaction to small movements.

This closes the open item above — "morphology, behavioural contingency, or both?" — in favour of contingency, from a source with no stake in the answer, and it matches the wiki's own indirect evidence.

It is also directly buildable. mecanumbot-perception already tracks people and body pose; gaze-like orientation and motion-contingent response are within reach on robot-platform. Contingent responsiveness may be a cheaper route to attribution than expressive fidelity is — and it is measurable in validation-study as a manipulation: same expressions, contingent versus delayed/random response.

Synthesis drawn here. OCC does not cite the HRI literature; the HRI sources in this corpus do not cite OCC.

appraisal-theory adds a mechanism for the same phenomenon from the other end: reverse appraisal — observers infer an agent's goals and standards backwards from its emotional displays (ortony-2022-cognitive-structure p. 243). On that account attribution is not projection but inference, which implies a robot whose displays are inconsistent with its actions teaches observers a value system it does not have.
