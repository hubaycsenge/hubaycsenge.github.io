This is almost certainly reference [1] of research-proposal — it is the paper that names and defines ethorobotics, and it is co-authored by Korondi, who supervises the Hungarian robotics side of this literature. Treat the identification as strong but not confirmed until Csenge verifies.

The theoretical foundation of the thesis. Peer-reviewed, widely cited.

Argument

  1. Social robotics is misguided in chasing human likeness. Mori's uncanny valley is the only serious hypothesis about human–robot affinity, and it predicts the opposite of the field's intuition (uncanny-valley).
  2. Reinterpreted ethologically, the valley is not robot-specific. It reflects a general biological need to categorise other agents — conspecific vs heterospecific, familiar vs unfamiliar, individual vs group member. Mori's "Medium Peak" is really interaction with heterospecifics that share many human attributes (e.g. domesticated animals); the "Maximum Peak" is conspecifics. Conspecifics are judged far more selectively, which is why the Maximum Peak has a narrower base.
  3. The paradox: passing the valley does not solve anything, because technology will keep going. Robots that reach and exceed human performance fall into a second uncanny valley on the far side. The landscape is symmetric. Convergence on humanness should be replaced by divergence.
  4. The escape: go around the peak. Determine the robot's function and environment first and design the best-suited agent regardless of human similarity — function-orientedness. Ethologically, the robot occupies its own niche.
  5. Dogs demonstrate this works. Domestication (~18,800–32,100 years) produced an agent that is morphologically and behaviourally very different from humans yet has enough social-competence to integrate into human society. Breeds occupy specific behavioural niches. Both species retained independent capacity to evolve; competition is limited (dog-human-interaction-model).

Key definitions

  • Social competence — "an individual's ability to generate social skills that conform to the expectations of others and the social rules of the group." Emerges only if the individual wants to participate, has the means, and is regarded by others as able to participate.
  • Partnership is a process, not a state. "A social robot does not automatically become a social partner but achieves this state of affairs if it engages in the appropriate kind of social interactions." Not an a priori attribute of the robot (interspecific-hri).

Dog-derived skills proposed for robots: attachment to humans, simple communicative interaction, responsiveness to learning and training, and being useful in some specific way.

Why the alternatives fail

The paper rejects the "expose infants to robots early" route explicitly — it is unrealistic, unethical, and risks confusing the human social recognition system, potentially producing misguided social and sexual preferences later in life.

Limitations

  • A theoretical paper. It proposes a research programme and closes by listing open questions, not results. The empirical work is elsewhere (gacsi-2016-emotion-attribution, lakatos-2014-emotion-attribution).
  • Says nothing about emotion models or architecture — no Panksepp, no Plutchik, no engine. It licenses an affective layer but does not specify one.
  • The niche concept is used analogically; it is not operationalised into design criteria a robot engineer could test against.
