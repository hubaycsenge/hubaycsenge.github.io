Can a robot have emotions?

Why this page exists. ortony-2022-cognitive-structure states flatly that robots "are simply not the kinds of things that can be conscious" and therefore cannot have emotions (p. 219). The thesis builds a robot emotional engine. That is a head-on collision with the authors of the model affective computing is built on, and it is a question a defence committee can ask in one sentence.

The corpus now holds three positions on it. This page records them, states which the thesis needs, and — importantly — argues that the thesis needs less than it might appear.

The three positions

Position Source Claim Held?
No — machines cannot have emotions ortony-2022-cognitive-structure p. 219 Emotion requires subjective experience; experience requires consciousness; only biological entities can be conscious ✅ primary
Yes — robots will have emotions Sloman & Croucher (1981) Cited by OCC as the opposing camp; also Frijda & Swagerman (1987), Adolphs & Anderson (2018) ❌ unheld
Affect is a neurodynamic property of evolved circuits panksepp-affective-systems Affect is the subjective experiential-feeling component of specific subcortical systems; dual-aspect monism ✅ primary, but does not address machines

A fourth, and the one the thesis should probably occupy, is implicit in plutchik-model — see below.

What OCC actually claims, and what it does not

The argument on p. 219 is a chain, and each link is separable:

  1. The subjective experience of emotion is central (to OCC's subject matter).
  2. Computers cannot have experience unless they are conscious.
  3. Only biological entities can be conscious — offered explicitly as a belief, "perhaps naïvely".
  4. Therefore computational artifacts cannot have emotions.

Three things are worth noticing.

It is a claim about phenomenal experience, not about function. OCC does not deny that a machine can compute appraisals, maintain affective state variables, or let those variables shape action selection. Its own Ch. 9 formalism does exactly that. What it denies is that anything it is like something to be is happening.

The authors immediately affirm the engineering programme. In the same paragraph: "our skepticism over the possibility of machines having emotions certainly does not mean that we think the topic of emotion is not relevant for the design of intelligent computational devices; on the contrary, we think it is an important topic that is becoming more important by the day" — naming robotics and HCI among the application areas.

Premise 3 is asserted, not argued, and the authors flag it as such. It is a substantive position in philosophy of mind (biological naturalism) presented in one clause of a psychology monograph. It should be reported as their stance, not as a finding.

Contradiction — but narrower than it looks

OCC denies that robots can experience emotions. This thesis needs robots to generate, maintain and act on affective state. These are not the same claim.

The collision is real only if the thesis asserts phenomenal experience. If it asserts a functional architecture, OCC's position is compatible with it — and OCC's own formalism becomes usable rather than off-limits.

Analysis drawn here, not stated by any source. But it is the analysis the thesis should adopt, because it is both defensible and cheap.

What the thesis actually needs to claim

The novelty claim in research-proposal and thesis is about real-time event detection in real environments and fusing human affect with the robot's own internal state. Neither requires phenomenal consciousness. The engine needs to:

  • estimate a human's affective state from perception,
  • maintain internal variables reflecting its own condition,
  • combine these into a state that determines expressive and navigational behaviour,
  • and do so fast enough, in the wild.

Every one of those is a functional claim, and every one is empirically testable. None of them is touched by OCC's objection.

The recommendation for the thesis: adopt functional language explicitly and early, and say so in the introduction. Write "the robot's affective state" rather than "the robot's feelings"; "generates an emotional response" rather than "experiences an emotion". This costs nothing, forecloses a whole line of viva questioning, and is what function-orientedness already commits the thesis to on independent grounds.

Plutchik supplies the vocabulary for exactly this

plutchik-model distinguishes three languages for emotion — subjective, behavioural, and functional — and builds his model in the functional one. That is the move the thesis needs, already made, by a source it already holds and already favours for the state set.

Plutchik's functional language describes an emotion by what it does — its adaptive role in the organism's interaction with its environment — with no commitment about what it feels like. A robot can instantiate a functional emotion without any phenomenal claim. This is also why Plutchik's behavioural column is implementable on a faceless robot while Panksepp's circuits are not directly.

The three models line up on this question in a useful way

  • OCC gives the appraisal front end and denies experience. Compatible with a functional engine; its own formalism is functional.
  • Plutchik gives the functional language and the behaviour mapping. Designed to be neutral about experience.
  • Panksepp grounds affect in evolved neural circuits and is the one genuinely awkward for a robot — his affects are the subjective-feeling aspect of specific subcortical neurodynamics, so a robot with no such substrate has no affect on his account. The corpus does not record him addressing machines directly, so this is inference, not citation. #needs-source

This slightly weakens the case for Panksepp-as-architecture on emotion-model-comparison — not on grounds of usefulness, but because borrowing his architecture while discarding his grounding needs an explicit justification the wiki has not yet written.

Ethorobotics has an independent answer

ethorobotics argues social robots should be treated as a separate species adapted to their niche, with human similarity irrelevant except where function requires it. Applied here: asking whether a robot's emotions are really emotions, by a standard derived from human phenomenal experience, is the same category error as asking whether its locomotion is really walking.

The functional question — does this state do for the robot what emotion does for a social animal, namely regulate interaction adaptively — is the one ethorobotics licenses. And it is answerable by experiment.

Note the asymmetry this produces, which is genuinely in the thesis' favour: emotion-attribution shows that humans attribute emotions to robots regardless, accurately and consistently, and act on the attribution. Whether the robot "has" emotions is philosophically contested; whether people respond to it as though it does is settled empirical fact in this corpus. The thesis is validated on the second, not the first.

Open

  • Sloman & Croucher (1981), Frijda & Swagerman (1987), Adolphs & Anderson (2018) are all unheld. The first would give the strongest counter-argument to OCC's position, from within AI. Filed to citation-backlog.
  • Does Panksepp anywhere address artificial systems? Not in the two papers held. #needs-source
  • Should this argument appear in the thesis introduction or in a dedicated section? An introduction paragraph is probably enough, given that the functional framing makes the objection dissolve rather than require rebuttal.
