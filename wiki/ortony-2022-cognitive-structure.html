Andrew Ortony (Northwestern), Gerald L. Clore (Virginia) and Allan Collins (Northwestern), The Cognitive Structure of Emotions, 2nd edition, Cambridge University Press, 2022. First edition 1988; first paperback 1990; reprinted 1990, 1994. DOI 10.1017/9781108934053.

The OCC model, held at last as a primary. citation-backlog listed it as "a third emotion model in the corpus alongside Panksepp and Plutchik, and the one game AI actually uses" — reached until now only second-hand through belle-2019-programming-with-affect. It is the source of the emotion set behind EMoBeT, GAMYGDALA and much of affective computing.

This is the second edition, and the difference is not cosmetic

The wiki has been treating "OCC" as the 1988 model, because that is what belle-2019-programming-with-affect cites. The 2022 edition changes four things that matter here: 22 → 24 emotion types; "eliciting conditions" replaced by "emergence conditions", a deliberate move away from treating emotions as things waiting to be elicited toward a constructivist view of them as emergent and often unwittingly constructed; a substantially expanded treatment of intensity; and a new contributed chapter on affective computing by Jonathan Gratch and Stacy Marsella (Ch. 10). Any claim in the wiki sourced to "OCC 1988" via a secondary citation should be checked against this edition before it is reused.

Takeaways

  • Three construals, three value systems, three central variables (Fig. 2.1, p. 29). Emotions arise from focusing on events, appraised against goals by their desirability; on agents' actions, appraised against standards by their praiseworthiness; or on objects, appraised against tastes by their appeal. See appraisal-theory.
  • 24 emotion types in six groups, differentiated by emergence conditions — the necessary but not sufficient conditions distinguishing one type from another. Enumerated on occ-model.
  • Emotion types are not emotion words. The labels ("Joy", "Schadenfreude") are explicitly stated to be suggestive placeholders for structural descriptions, not definitions. The theory is about "the emotions that characterize the human condition, rather than the words referring to those emotions in English or any other language" (Ch. 2). This is a stronger position on the language problem than either panksepp-affective-systems or plutchik-model takes.
  • Intensity is first-class, and separated into global and local variables (Ch. 4). Four global variables affect intensity across all groups: sense-of-reality, psychological proximity, unexpectedness, and pre-existing arousal (p. 72 ff.). Local variables act within particular groups — e.g. effort and familiarity. Both typically exert their influence through the central variables rather than beside them.
  • A two-stage computational formalism with an explicit threshold (Ch. 9, pp. 215–228). Recorded in full on occ-model; relevant to emotion-arbitration.
  • The Attraction group is conditioned on the object being construed as emotion-capable — and the authors name domestic robots and dogs as the cases. This is the finding with the most direct bearing on this thesis; see below.

Objects that are "emotion-capable" — the passage that matters here

Chapter 8 splits the Attraction emotions by whether the object is implicitly viewed by the experiencer as being capable or incapable of experiencing emotions (Table 8.1, p. 180):

emotion-capable object emotion-incapable object
appealing "Affection" (adore, like, love) "Appreciation"
unappealing "Enmity" (despise, dislike, hate) "Distaste"

Objects that are viewed as being capable of emotional experience are typically animate, while those viewed as being incapable of emotional experience are usually inanimate, the exception being that people sometimes personify certain kinds of inanimate objects such as houseplants and domestic robots. (p. 180)

The criterion is then made explicit: "Affection" emotions "are contingent on a view of the object, however misguided, as being somehow capable of at least the possibility of some reciprocity of feeling or concern" (p. 180). The worked contrast is a dog against a pet skunk — the dog qualifies because it is "highly responsive to others", exchanges gaze, and "respond[s] to even slight movements on [its] owner's part"; the skunk does not, being "remarkably oblivious to humans, never exchanging gazes".

An independent, and unusually specific, statement of the thesis' premise

A cognitive-psychology theory with no stake in robotics arrives at emotion-attribution from the other direction, and supplies something the ethology literature does not: a criterion. What moves an object from "Appreciation" to "Affection" is not similarity to a human, or even animacy — it is responsiveness and apparent reciprocity, evidenced by gaze exchange and contingent reaction to small movements.

That is function-orientedness stated in emotion-theory terms, and it is close to a build specification for robot-platform — which already has the gaze and motion-contingency machinery (mecanumbot-perception). It also supplies a mechanism for why the dog is the right model species (dog-human-interaction-model): dogs are the paradigm case in OCC's own text.

Synthesis drawn here; OCC does not cite the ethorobotics literature and the ethorobotics literature in this corpus does not cite OCC.

Where OCC contradicts the thesis

Both contradictions come from Ch. 9 §Computational Tractability, and both are stated by the authors in their own voice. Neither is resolved here — see machine-emotion-question.

Contradiction — OCC's authors deny that a robot can have emotions at all

"Our view … is that the subjective experience of emotion is central, and we do not consider it possible for computers to have any kind of experience, emotional or otherwise, until and unless they are capable of being conscious. Because we believe, perhaps naïvely, that only biological entities are capable of being conscious, and because machines are not biological entities we believe that computational artifacts, be they robots or virtual characters, are simply not the kinds of things that can be conscious." (p. 219)

OCC is built to let a machine predict and explain what emotions a human would feel — "not 'have' them" (Ch. 2). This thesis builds an engine that generates the robot's own affective state from human affect plus internal state (emotion-engine).

The authors cite the opposing camp rather than dismissing it: Sloman & Croucher (1981), Why robots will have emotions; Adolphs & Anderson (2018); Frijda & Swagerman (1987). All three are unheld — see citation-backlog.

Note the position is narrower than it first reads: they explicitly deny only experience, and immediately affirm that emotion is "an important topic" for robotics and HCI. A functional engine that makes no phenomenal claim may not actually be in conflict. That is the question machine-emotion-question exists to settle.

Contradiction — OCC prefers hand-coding to learning

"While it is possible that machine learning techniques could lead to the development of these prerequisites, we believe that hand-coding the kinds of representations and cognition–emotion relationships that we have outlined is likely to be at least as effective." (p. 219)

Directly against rl-for-behaviour-learning and step 7 of thesis. The claim is offered as a belief, with no supporting evidence or comparison, in a book whose empirical content is about human emotion rather than about learning algorithms — so it is weakly supported on its own terms. But it is the same position reached in practice by szabo-2011-emotional-engine and belle-2019-programming-with-affect, both of which hand-authored every rule. The corpus now contains three independent instances of the hand-coding choice and none of the learning choice. That is worth confronting rather than dismissing.

Chapter 10 — Gratch & Marsella on OCC and affective computing

A contributed chapter by two authors of EMA, reviewing OCC's downstream influence. It is effectively a free literature review of computational appraisal, and opens several citation frontiers the wiki lacks.

  • The lineage: Elliott's Affective Reasoner (a direct translation of OCC's mappings into software) → Gratch's Émile (deriving appraisal variables from general AI planning representations rather than hand-authored rules) → EMA (Gratch & Marsella), a domain-independent model of appraisal and coping, realised in the SOAR cognitive architecture (pp. 233–241).
  • Upstream vs downstream (pp. 235–239). Upstream work asks how a machine derives appraisal variables from goals and world state; downstream work asks how affective states then shape decisions and action. OCC itself specifies only the mapping between them — "affect derivation" and "affect intensity".
  • Appraisal as a by-product of competent agency. Gratch and Marsella argue appraisals "summarize key judgments that any intelligent system would need to make to act adaptively in the world" — goal congruence, likelihood, norm compliance. On that reading an appraisal layer is not an addition to a robot architecture but a reading-off of quantities a capable agent already computes. Directly relevant to Q2: it suggests the same argument may extend to internal state.
  • EMA links appraisal to coping through control: high perceived control selects problem-focused coping, low control selects emotion-focused coping (p. 239). A concrete arbitration mechanism, and a different one from emotion-arbitration's current candidates.
  • "Reverse appraisal" (p. 243): observers infer an agent's goals and standards backwards from its emotional displays. Demonstrated to shift human decisions in social tasks. This is a mechanism for why expressive behaviour works at all, and it applies to a robot — the chapter names Emys and Glin, card-playing robots whose displayed emotions let human players infer their dispositions.

Chapter 10 supplies an argument the perception gap needs

"Much of affective computing focuses on recognizing a person's emotions from surface cues alone, yet external expressions have only a loose connection to internal states" (p. 242, citing L. F. Barrett et al. and Ortony & Turner). Observers do better when they know the immediately preceding events; OCC supplies exactly that knowledge.

multimodal-emotion-recognition frames the gap as no source says how to read affect off a body pose. This source reframes it: pose alone is the wrong target, and no amount of skeleton data closes it. Accurate affect inference needs the situation and the person's goals as well. That is a harder problem than the wiki has been assuming — but it also means the platform's context and event history are not incidental, they are load-bearing.

Method and limitations

  • A theoretical monograph, not an empirical report. Its own supporting studies are described only in passing in Ch. 2 — an automated-diary study (Turner) and a field study of basketball fans, the latter reported as recovering the three proposed classes. Neither is presented with numbers in the text; treat the empirical support as asserted rather than demonstrated in this volume.
  • No inter-rater or predictive validation of the 24-type taxonomy is given here. The change from 22 to 24 types is offered by the authors themselves as evidence "that the theory can be readily modified" — which cuts both ways.
  • The theory is entirely cognitive. No neural grounding, no evolutionary argument, no physiological correlates — the opposite methodological commitment from panksepp-affective-systems, and unlike plutchik-model it makes no claim to adaptive function as the organising principle.
  • Anthropocentric by construction. Goals, standards and tastes are human value systems; the Attribution group presumes responsibility attribution. Whether any of it transfers to an interspecific-hri setting is untested and unasked — the same concern Q5 in open-questions raises about the affective-computing tradition generally.
  • Digits do not extract from this PDF. pdftotext silently drops every Arabic numeral. All page numbers, years and figure numbers on this page were read from the rendered page images instead. Anyone citing further claims from this source must do the same — do not trust a page number taken from the extracted text.
