Navigation strategies for Deep3R-based object search

Python ROS 2 Nav2 behaviour trees search algorithms

Description

The robot has to find an object in a room it has seen before. In a first visit it explores the room while a GPU server builds a 3D reconstruction of it from the robot's camera stream (the Deep3R pipeline). Later, in a second visit, it is given a target — named in words — and has to find it, even if the object has been moved in the meantime.

This task is about how the robot moves during both visits: which exploration strategy produces a map good enough to search in, when to stop exploring, how to return to where the object was last seen, and how to search when it is not there any more. Several strategies are implemented, run side by side in simulation and on the robot, and compared.

Background — why we do it

Object search is a classic problem in active perception, and animals are very good at it. A dog that sees a toy hidden and then taken away first goes to where it last saw the toy, and then searches around that spot — searching intensively near the last evidence and widening out as time passes, a pattern ethology calls area-restricted search. Foraging theory adds a principled answer to when to give up on an area. Robotic search, by contrast, usually relies on frontier-based exploration or on full coverage, which treat every unexplored cell alike.

On the technological side, the robot's 2D LiDAR sees only one horizontal plane, while the server-side 3D reconstruction sees tabletops, chair legs and the target object itself. The navigation strategy decides which views the reconstruction gets, so it directly affects the quality of the map, and through it whether the object can be found at all. Localisation errors are also known to be mistaken for "the object has moved", so a good strategy relocalises before it concludes that something is missing. The project is part of my PhD work on a search behaviour modelled on the motivational (SEEKING) system of mammals.

Requirements for the finished system

  • At least three navigation strategies implemented as interchangeable behaviour-tree subtrees on top of Nav2 — for example a coverage/frontier baseline, go-to-last-seen-location, and area-restricted search spreading outward from the last-seen position.
  • An explicit stopping rule for the exploration phase and an explicit giving-up rule for the search phase, both configurable by parameter.
  • Integration with the existing server link: the strategies consume the server's map updates and found / not-found announcements, and switch between the exploration and search phases through the existing protocol.
  • A relocalisation check before the robot treats the target as absent from its remembered location.
  • A reproducible evaluation protocol in simulation (fixed rooms, object placements and random seeds) that logs, per run: time to find, path length, area covered, and whether the object was found or wrongly declared missing.
  • A comparison of the strategies over repeated simulated runs, followed by a smaller set of runs on the real robot in the lab.
  • Documentation: launch instructions, parameter reference, and a short report of the comparison.

A learned fear response for MecanumBot in human–robot interaction

Python reinforcement learning Gym-style environments ROS 2 behaviour trees

Description

Dogs that are startled freeze, back away, or turn to their owner for reassurance — and they stay cautious for a while after the threat is gone. This task builds that behaviour into MecanumBot in an interaction scenario: the robot carries out a simple delivery task while a person moves around it, and a person coming too close or too fast raises an internal fear level that decays slowly afterwards.

A reinforcement-learning agent learns when to freeze, avoid (retreat with Nav2), catch the person's attention, or carry on with the task. A first version of the fear dynamics, a training environment and draft behaviour-tree leaves exist; the work is to connect them to real LiDAR input, bring the learned policy into the robot's behaviour tree, and test it in the MuJoCo digital twin and on the robot.

Background — why we do it

In animals, fear is not a reflex that stops when the stimulus disappears: defensive states build up with threat and fade slowly, and the choice between freezing, fleeing and seeking social support depends on the situation and on learning. A robot with a comparable internal state behaves differently from one with a plain safety rule — a proximity-based slow-down returns to full speed the moment a person steps back, whereas a fear-modulated robot remains cautious. Whether people read this residual caution as an emotion, and whether it makes the robot easier to predict, is an ethorobotics question.

Reinforcement learning is a natural fit because which defensive response pays off depends on the consequences — a retreat that costs the task, a freeze that prevents a collision — rather than on a hand-written rule. Keeping the learned policy inside a behaviour tree keeps the robot's behaviour inspectable and safe, which matters when it runs next to people in an experiment.

Requirements for the finished system

  • A fear model driven by the robot's real /scan data: risk from the minimum range and closing speed of people or obstacles, a spike on near-collision, and a slow, parameterised decay.
  • A Gym-style training environment with the discrete actions freeze, avoid, catch attention and task, and the fear level in the observation.
  • A trained policy compared against a fixed hand-written heuristic on task completion, near-collisions and how long caution persists after the person leaves.
  • A ROS 2 behaviour-tree package in which the policy selects between the four responses, with avoid implemented as a Nav2 retreat and freeze cancelling navigation.
  • Runs of the full stack in the MuJoCo digital twin, then on the real robot in the lab with a person present.
  • Every parameter of the fear dynamics and the reward documented, with its biological motivation — these are experimental design choices, reviewed with an ethologist before any study.
  • Unit tests for the fear dynamics and the reward, and launch instructions for simulation and robot.

Effects of visual distractors on motor performance (Fitts' task)

3 students

Python pygame or web front end experimental design statistics machine learning

Description

An investigation of how distracting visual (LED) signals degrade human motor performance. The project involves implementing a test application (pygame-based or web-based) with full interaction logging, designing the distracting effects and at least two test conditions with an appropriately disturbing signal, and running a human experiment. The results are evaluated with statistical analysis and with an AI/ML model trained on the collected data.

Background — why we do it

Fitts' law is one of the most robust quantitative laws of human movement: the time needed to point at a target grows with the logarithm of the ratio of distance to target width — the index of difficulty. Because the relationship is so stable, Fitts' task is the standard tool for measuring how interfaces, input devices or conditions change motor performance, summarised as throughput.

Visual attention research shows that sudden onsets, flicker and salient colours in the periphery capture attention even when they are irrelevant to the task. This matters for robots: a robot in a shared space often communicates with lights — status LEDs, signals meant to attract a person or show where to go. The same signal that makes a robot understandable may disrupt what a person is doing. Measuring the cost of such signals on a well-understood motor task gives a quantitative basis for designing robot light signals that inform without disturbing.

Requirements for the finished system

  • A Fitts' task application (pygame or web) with configurable target distances and widths, covering a range of indices of difficulty.
  • Full interaction logging: timestamped pointer trajectory, target parameters, hits and misses, movement time, and the distractor state at every moment, saved in an analysis-ready format.
  • A distractor module that drives the LED signals (or a faithful on-screen substitute) synchronised with the task log, with parameters such as position, colour, flash frequency and onset timing.
  • At least two distractor conditions plus a no-distractor control, with a written rationale for why each signal should be disturbing.
  • An experimental design: within-subject conditions, counterbalanced order, practice trials, target number of participants, informed consent, and anonymised data.
  • The experiment carried out with human participants.
  • Statistical analysis: Fitts' law fits per condition, and a comparison of movement time, error rate and throughput between conditions with an appropriate repeated-measures or mixed-effects model.
  • An AI/ML model on the collected data — for example predicting the condition from trajectory features, or predicting movement time — with a proper participant-wise train/test split.
  • A reproducible repository and a short report of the method and results.

2D ground-plane projection from the laboratory camera system

3 students

Python OpenCV camera calibration projective geometry

Description

Creation of a common top-down (2D) view of a laboratory room from the images of the fixed camera system installed there. The project involves calibrating the cameras, computing the image-to-ground-plane homography for each camera, and fusing the individual views into a single, metrically consistent 2D map of the laboratory floor. The resulting projection can be used to localise and track objects and beings — people, dogs, robots — moving in the lab.

Background — why we do it

Behavioural experiments with people, dogs and robots are recorded on video and then coded by hand: who was where, how close, moving in which direction. A top-down, metric view of the floor turns those recordings into positions in centimetres, which makes automatic tracking, distance measurements and trajectory analysis possible, and gives a ground truth for the robot's own localisation.

Technologically, every point on a flat floor is related to its image in a camera by a homography, a 3×3 projective transformation. Estimating it needs the camera's intrinsic parameters and lens distortion (from a checkerboard or ChArUco calibration) and a set of floor points with known positions. Once each camera is mapped to the same floor coordinate system, the views can be stitched into one map. The homography is exact only for points on the floor, which is why tracking uses the foot point of a person or animal and why the achievable accuracy has to be measured rather than assumed.

Requirements for the finished system

  • A documented, reproducible calibration procedure: intrinsic and distortion calibration for every camera, and floor markers placed at measured positions in a common lab coordinate system.
  • An image-to-ground-plane homography for each camera, stored in a configuration file together with its calibration data.
  • A fused top-down map of the lab floor at a fixed metric resolution (for example 1 px = 1 cm), handling overlapping fields of view.
  • Measured accuracy: projection error in centimetres at check points that were not used for calibration, reported for each camera and in the overlaps.
  • A projection pipeline running on the live camera streams at a usable frame rate, not only on stored images.
  • A function or small API that converts an image point from any camera into floor coordinates, as the interface for later tracking work.
  • Minimum expected outcome: a working, reproducible calibration procedure and a projection pipeline running on the live camera streams.

Custom-criteria path planning for MecanumBot (Nav2)

3 students

Python ROS 2 Nav2 C++ web front end

Description

Route planning and navigation for a mecanum-wheeled robot according to selectable criteria — for example shortest path, smoothest trajectory, distance kept from obstacles, or energy- or time-optimal routes. The project involves understanding the ROS 2 Nav2 navigation stack, implementing the criteria as a custom Nav2 path planner plugin, and testing it first in simulation and then on the real robot. As a final step, a simple web interface is developed on which the planning criteria can be selected and switched, and the resulting routes compared.

Background — why we do it

Standard planners optimise one thing — usually path length on a costmap — but "the best route" depends on the situation. Near people a robot should keep its distance; with a heavy load it should avoid sharp turns; on battery it should save energy. For the social robots in our experiments, the shape of a route is part of the behaviour people observe and interpret, so being able to choose and compare route criteria is useful beyond efficiency.

A mecanum-wheeled robot is holonomic: it can drive sideways and diagonally without turning. This widens the set of possible paths, but the motions are not equally cheap — sideways and diagonal driving typically loses more energy to roller slip than driving forward — so time and energy criteria differ from path length in ways a differential-drive planner never has to consider. Nav2 exposes global planning through a plugin interface, so new criteria can be added without changing the rest of the navigation stack.

Requirements for the finished system

  • A custom Nav2 global planner plugin (Nav2's planner plugin interface is C++; the cost criteria can be prototyped in Python first) that plans on the robot's costmap and supports at least four criteria: shortest, smoothest, maximum clearance, and energy- or time-optimal using a motion-cost model of the mecanum base.
  • Criteria that can be selected and switched at runtime, through parameters, without restarting the navigation stack.
  • A metric report for every planned route: length, total curvature or number of turns, minimum obstacle clearance, estimated time and energy, and planning time.
  • Tests in simulation on fixed maps and goal sets, then runs on the real robot in the lab, with the planned and the driven paths recorded.
  • A web interface that shows the map, lets the user choose a criterion and send a goal, and overlays the routes of different criteria with their metrics for comparison.
  • Launch files, a parameter reference and a short evaluation report.

Should I ask or should I go? Trust in a robotic arm

3 students

interest in psychology computer vision robot programming Python

Description

Experimental testing of people's trust in a robotic arm in different scenarios: correct behaviour without asking for information, mistakes without asking for information, and asking for information to different extents. The project covers the complete research cycle — reviewing psychological questionnaires, designing the experiment, programming the robot, and running the experiments. The work is supported from the psychological side by two cognitive science students, and the topic is co-supervised.

Background — why we do it

People rely on robots only as much as they trust them, and appropriate trust — not simply more trust — is what makes collaboration safe: over-trust leads people to follow a faulty robot, under-trust leads them to ignore a reliable one. Research on trust in automation and in human–robot interaction shows that robot errors reduce trust, and that the robot's performance is among the strongest factors shaping it.

A robot can also avoid errors by asking — confirming which object is meant, or whether a step is correct. Asking may make the robot appear careful and trustworthy, or incompetent and tiresome, and the balance probably depends on how often it asks. Dogs face the same dilemma with their owners: they look back at the human when a situation is uncertain. Measuring how the amount of inquiry trades off against mistakes tells designers when a robot should ask and when it should just go.

Requirements for the finished system

  • A literature review of trust in human–robot interaction and of validated questionnaires for measuring it, ending with the chosen measures.
  • An experimental design with at least the three scenario types — correct behaviour without inquiry, mistakes without inquiry, and inquiry at two or more different frequencies — including the task, the hypotheses, the participant allocation, and informed consent.
  • A robotic arm program for the experimental task that performs the task correctly, commits scripted mistakes at controlled moments, and asks for information in the inquiry conditions, all triggered identically across participants.
  • Computer vision for the task — for example detecting and localising the objects the arm handles — robust enough that unplanned errors do not contaminate the conditions.
  • Logging of the robot's actions, its questions, the participant's answers and timing, synchronised with the questionnaire data.
  • The experiment carried out with participants, followed by a statistical comparison of trust between scenarios and a short report written together with the cognitive science students.
