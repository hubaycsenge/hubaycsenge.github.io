Effects of visual distractors on motor performance (Fitts' task)

Python pygame or web front end experimental design statistics machine learning

Ask about this topic on Teams →

Description

An investigation of how distracting visual (LED) signals degrade human motor performance. The project involves implementing a test application (pygame-based or web-based) with full interaction logging, designing the distracting effects and at least two test conditions with an appropriately disturbing signal, and running a human experiment. The results are evaluated with statistical analysis and with an AI/ML model trained on the collected data.

Background — why we do it

Fitts' law is one of the most robust quantitative laws of human movement: the time needed to point at a target grows with the logarithm of the ratio of distance to target width — the index of difficulty. Because the relationship is so stable, Fitts' task is the standard tool for measuring how interfaces, input devices or conditions change motor performance, summarised as throughput.

Visual attention research shows that sudden onsets, flicker and salient colours in the periphery capture attention even when they are irrelevant to the task. This matters for robots: a robot in a shared space often communicates with lights — status LEDs, signals meant to attract a person or show where to go. The same signal that makes a robot understandable may disrupt what a person is doing. Measuring the cost of such signals on a well-understood motor task gives a quantitative basis for designing robot light signals that inform without disturbing.

Requirements for the finished system

  • A Fitts' task application (pygame or web) with configurable target distances and widths, covering a range of indices of difficulty.
  • Full interaction logging: timestamped pointer trajectory, target parameters, hits and misses, movement time, and the distractor state at every moment, saved in an analysis-ready format.
  • A distractor module that drives the LED signals (or a faithful on-screen substitute) synchronised with the task log, with parameters such as position, colour, flash frequency and onset timing.
  • At least two distractor conditions plus a no-distractor control, with a written rationale for why each signal should be disturbing.
  • An experimental design: within-subject conditions, counterbalanced order, practice trials, target number of participants, informed consent, and anonymised data.
  • The experiment carried out with human participants.
  • Statistical analysis: Fitts' law fits per condition, and a comparison of movement time, error rate and throughput between conditions with an appropriate repeated-measures or mixed-effects model.
  • An AI/ML model on the collected data — for example predicting the condition from trajectory features, or predicting movement time — with a proper participant-wise train/test split.
  • A reproducible repository and a short report of the method and results.
